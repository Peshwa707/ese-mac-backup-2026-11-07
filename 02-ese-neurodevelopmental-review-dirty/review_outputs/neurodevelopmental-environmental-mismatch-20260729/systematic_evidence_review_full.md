# Adaptive-Constraint and Environmental-Mismatch Model of Adult Neurodevelopmental Functioning: AI-Assisted Rapid Systematic Evidence Review Draft

**Final search date:** 2026-07-29

> **GOVERNANCE STATUS — NOT CLINICALLY SIGNED OFF**  
> This is an AI Reviewer‑1 rapid systematic evidence-review draft generated with the local Evidence Synthesis Engine. Independent human Reviewer 2 screening/adjudication and treating-clinician sign-off remain pending. It is not a diagnosis, treatment recommendation, or official human-gated engine export.

## Abstract

**Objective:** Adversarially test the atomic claims, counterclaims, and global null hypothesis in a proposed adaptive-constraint/environmental-mismatch formulation of adult neurodevelopmental functioning.

**Methods:** Seven evidence modules were prospectively compiled from the supplied protocol. PubMed/MEDLINE and Europe PMC were searched live from inception through 29 July 2026; ClinicalTrials.gov was also queried for scaffolding, sleep/circadian, and mood/substance modules. The live engine identified 4,165 module-record occurrences, retained 3,770 after within-module deduplication, and verified 3,638. A rapid cap advanced 980 records to one AI title/abstract reviewer; 2,658 verified records were deferred rather than excluded. A targeted PubMed gap search and official NICE guideline inspection supplemented specific safety and bias gaps. Selected evidence was extracted only after abstract or official-guideline inspection. No new meta-analysis was performed because populations, exposures, constructs, and outcomes were heterogeneous.

**Results:** AI Reviewer‑1 classified 206 records as include, 27 uncertain, and 747 exclude across module occurrences. A bounded set of 98 extracted occurrences (92 unique PMIDs before adding two guidelines) informed claim-level synthesis. The strongest support concerned developmental persistence and multi-setting impairment in ADHD, social/peer burden, bidirectional sleep/state relevance, and cannabis-related psychiatric risk. Evidence was weaker or indirect for intelligence as masking, a unitary environmental-mismatch mechanism, DCD overlap in adults, durable AI scaffolding, generalized neurodevelopmental strengths, and emotional-compensation/temporal claims about daydreaming. The global null hypothesis was not rejected.

**Conclusions:** Components of the model are plausible, but the literature cannot show that one coherent model explains this individual better than bipolar-spectrum state, sleep, substances/medications, anxiety/trauma, burnout, learned behavior, recall bias, or multiple interacting causes. Prospective clinical and collateral data are required.

## Executive findings

1. **Most defensible:** childhood ADHD can persist into adulthood and impair multiple settings; social and functional burden is real but mechanistically nonspecific.
2. **Clinically consequential:** sleep/circadian state, bipolar episodes, cannabis/THC, stimulant agent/dose, nicotine, and medication context should be treated as time-varying differential variables.
3. **Plausible but weaker:** context and reward influence performance; structured behavioral supports can help; a plural trait-plus-state formulation is reasonable.
4. **Overstated without new data:** high intelligence as the cause of delayed recognition, achievement as proof of masking, creativity/pattern recognition as intrinsic neurodevelopmental strengths, and daydreaming as proven emotional compensation.
5. **Unresolved:** the proposed unified adaptive-constraint model does not currently outperform the global null or a multiple-cause formulation.
6. **Individual boundary:** no group-level source can diagnose the individual or prove that a reported exposure caused a reported state.

## Review question and scope

Among adolescents and adults with ADHD, autism-spectrum traits, developmental-coordination difficulties, bipolar disorder, or overlapping profiles, how do executive differences, compensatory strategies, environment/task fit, external supports, sleep/circadian state, mood, substances, medication, social adversity, and daydreaming relate to impairment and adaptive functioning?

The source protocol is a multi-module review program and a longitudinal case-formulation framework, not one homogeneous PICOS review. Therefore, this report uses narrative synthesis and does not pool across diagnoses, exposures, or constructs.

## Methods

### Protocol and atomic claim ledger

The supplied protocol was decomposed into 66 atomic hypotheses, qualifications, counterclaims, model claims, and boundaries (H1–H12 and G1–G6). The personal narrative was not used as scientific evidence and was not transmitted in retrieval queries.

### Sources and search

PubMed/MEDLINE and Europe PMC were executed for all modules. ClinicalTrials.gov was executed for D, E1, and E2. Current official NICE ADHD (NG87) and bipolar (CG185) pages were inspected in Chrome. Exact queries, counts, caps, supplemental searches, and citation-chaining results are in `search_strategy.md`.

Embase, PsycINFO, Web of Science, Scopus, Cochrane CENTRAL, CINAHL, ERIC, ProQuest, and WHO ICTRP were not searched directly in this run. Their use by included reviews does not count as a database search by this review.

### Deduplication and rapid cap

Verified identifiers were normalized and deduplicated within modules. Cross-module duplicates were retained because modules have different eligibility questions. Each module advanced 140 hierarchy-ranked records. Deferred records were not excluded.

### Screening and reviewer status

One AI Reviewer‑1 screened title/abstract records using module-specific criteria. There was no human Reviewer 2, no consensus reconciliation, and no completed full-text eligibility stage. The engine's clinical sign-off gate remains closed. These limitations lower the claim ceiling and preclude describing this as a complete systematic review.

### Source integrity

All 92 selected PMIDs were re-fetched from NCBI PubMed EFetch; all had abstracts. Exact titles were matched against local retrieval records. DOI URLs were resolved where present, with outcomes retained in `verified_references.csv`. PubMed retraction/correction relationships were checked in the returned XML. Two official guideline pages were inspected directly. Retrieval-stage unresolved records were excluded from evidence use.

### Extraction and synthesis

One structured row was created per selected source and exploded to one row per claim–source relationship in `claim_evidence_matrix.csv`. Numerical effects were recorded only when present in the inspected abstract; otherwise `NR` is used. Narrative synthesis was selected a priori for conceptual and clinical heterogeneity.

Each `Alternative explanation` entry is an explicitly unverified hypothesis to test, not an evidence-supported counterclaim, unless that entry itself cites a source. This distinction prevents uncited possibilities from being presented as established clinical facts.

### Risk of bias and certainty

Quality judgments are **provisional abstract-level appraisals**, not completed AMSTAR 2, RoB 2, ROBINS-I, QUIPS/JBI, QUADAS-2, or GRADE instruments. Judgments explicitly considered design, temporality, confounding, measurement validity, heterogeneity, precision, and directness. Formal instruments require full text and independent human checking; therefore, no claim receives a final clinical-certainty grade.

### Deviations from a complete review

- Rapid source and screening caps.
- One AI reviewer rather than two independent human reviewers.
- Abstract-level rather than complete full-text extraction for journal literature.
- No information-specialist PRESS review.
- Incomplete database coverage and citation chaining.
- No preregistration before retrieval.
- No formal full-text exclusions, complete design-specific risk-of-bias instruments, outcome-level GRADE, or statistical pooling.
- Three nonseparable pediatric or mixed-population sources (PMIDs 40868584, 42199316, and 42327684) were retained post hoc only as clearly labeled hypothesis-generating context. They do not contribute to claim verdicts; their frozen-criteria deviations are explicit in `claim_evidence_matrix.csv`.

## Risk-of-bias synthesis

- **Systematic reviews/meta-analyses:** often the strongest available evidence, but several had high heterogeneity, broad constructs, or observational inputs. Abstract-only review cannot support item-level AMSTAR 2 ratings.
- **Randomized trials:** strongest for bounded intervention and acute effects; often short, underpowered for rare mania/psychosis outcomes, and not designed for etiologic claims.
- **Longitudinal/within-person cohorts:** improve temporal inference but remain vulnerable to time-varying confounding, selection, attrition, and measurement change.
- **Cross-sectional and mediation studies:** useful for construct mapping; they cannot establish temporal direction even when labeled mediation.
- **Qualitative evidence:** useful for experience and perceived function; it does not establish prevalence, mechanism, or objective superiority.
- **Case applicability:** low for most claims because the literature cannot verify the individual's exposure timing, childhood onset, episode boundaries, or functional course.

### H1.1 — ADHD-related executive difficulties can begin in childhood

- **Verdict:** Supported.
- **Certainty:** Moderate.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct for childhood ADHD followed prospectively; less direct for executive difficulty specifically.
- **Directness to this case:** Low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** An 18-year cohort began with childhood ADHD and later measured neurocognition and broad functioning (PMID 42365682). A separate cohort measured childhood clinical/MRI features and adult functioning after 8.2 years (PMID 41985649).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** These abstracts establish childhood ADHD cohorts, not the exact onset date of each executive deficit. Neither shows that all adult executive problems were continuously present from childhood.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Learning disorder, sleep disruption, mood/anxiety, trauma, family or school context, medication effects, and neurological conditions can produce childhood executive complaints.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Childhood-onset ADHD can be associated with executive and functional difficulty that is detectable prospectively, but the onset and cause of a particular executive problem require contemporaneous developmental evidence.
- **Falsifier:** Repeated childhood records showing normal attention, organization, inhibition, and functioning across settings, followed by clearly new executive problems only after a later condition or exposure.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** School reports, parent/teacher ratings, developmental interview, early task-completion and organization history, learning evaluations, sleep history, and records predating mood, substance, trauma, medication, or occupational changes.

### H1.2 — ADHD-related executive difficulties can persist through adolescence

- **Verdict:** Partially supported.
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct in persistence reviews but indirect in the age-band study.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** A meta-analysis evaluated childhood ADHD persistence into later developmental stages (PMID 40801243). In a clinical age-band study, inattention was similar across ages while hyperactivity-impulsivity was lower in older groups (PMID 41716858).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** PMID 41716858 explicitly states that cross-sectional age patterns do not establish within-person persistence. The meta-analysis focused on comorbidity predictors rather than a pooled adolescent persistence rate.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Age-related change in symptom expression, treatment, informant expectations, school demands, and referral selection can mimic persistence or remission.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** ADHD symptoms and related impairment may continue into adolescence, often with changing symptom expression, but longitudinal multi-informant evidence is needed to establish persistence in an individual.
- **Falsifier:** High-quality longitudinal assessments showing childhood symptoms remit before adolescence with stable functioning and no later recurrence until a new exposure or disorder.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Repeated child-to-adolescent ratings, treatment timeline, teacher versus parent observations, school transitions, disciplinary and academic records, and changing environmental demands.

### H1.3 — ADHD-related executive difficulties can persist into adulthood

- **Verdict:** Supported.
- **Certainty:** Moderate.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct.
- **Directness to this case:** Low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Childhood ADHD predicted worse adult functioning after 18 years, with persistent ADHD worse than remitted ADHD on selected domains (PMID 42365682). Childhood ADHD cohorts also showed measurable adult function after 8.2 years (PMID 41985649). Older-adult evidence found attention and memory differences, although executive findings were mixed (PMID 41146423).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** Remitted ADHD groups existed and adult outcomes were heterogeneous. Executive findings in adults aged 50 years or older were inconsistent, and age-related differential diagnosis remains unresolved.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Adult sleep, mood, anxiety, substance use, personality pathology, medical/neurological disease, medication, and environmental load may independently cause or amplify executive problems.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Childhood ADHD can persist into adulthood and confer long-term functional risk, but persistence is heterogeneous and adult executive symptoms require competing explanations to be assessed.
- **Falsifier:** Prospective evidence showing no greater adult symptoms or functional impairment in childhood ADHD cohorts than matched controls after accounting for comorbidity and treatment.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Childhood onset evidence, adult diagnostic interview, current executive testing, collateral history, remission periods, treatment response, sleep and mood timeline, substance exposure, and neurological review.

### H1.4 — Persistent ADHD traits can impair more than one life setting

- **Verdict:** Supported.
- **Certainty:** Moderate.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct.
- **Directness to this case:** Low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** The 18-year cohort reported worse functioning on more than 60% of outcomes spanning academic/professional, adaptive, psychiatric, neurocognitive, physical-health, and service domains (PMID 42365682). Adult CBT evidence separately measured global, occupational, social, educational, and quality-of-life outcomes (PMID 41936228).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** Not every domain was impaired, effect sizes varied, and CBT findings show that functioning may change independently of symptom scores. Stigma evidence was mostly associative (PMID 42137527).
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Stigma, work or school mismatch, interpersonal conflict, mood/anxiety, personality pathology, pain, financial strain, and inadequate support can create cross-setting impairment.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Adult ADHD can be associated with impairment across multiple settings, but impairment should be measured separately in each setting and should not be inferred from symptom severity alone.
- **Falsifier:** Consistently intact functioning across education/work, home, relationships, self-care, and finances despite persistent symptoms, with no substantial compensatory burden.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Setting-by-setting impairment inventory, objective deadlines/errors, job and school history, relationship impact, financial/self-care functioning, supports used, and symptom-function concordance.

### H1.N1 — Adult difficulties may instead begin after mood, sleep, trauma, substance, medication, or occupational changes

- **Verdict:** Partially supported.
- **Certainty:** Moderate.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct for several modifiers, indirect for true adult onset.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Personality pathology predicted less functional improvement and more persistent symptoms during adult ADHD treatment (PMID 41906356). A medication-tolerance review found little evidence for common long-term therapeutic tolerance and recommended considering adherence, life events, symptom fluctuation, and co-occurring mental health conditions (PMID 41627718). Stigma was linked with worse function and quality of life (PMID 42137527).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** These studies mostly concern modifiers in people already identified with ADHD; they do not prove that adult difficulties began de novo from the alternative factor. Childhood comorbidity associations with persistence attenuated after adjustment (PMID 40801243).
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Both may be true: persistent neurodevelopmental vulnerability can interact with a later mood, sleep, trauma, substance, medication, medical, or workload change.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Later conditions and exposures can mimic or amplify ADHD-like impairment and must be timed explicitly; they should not automatically be treated as either the sole cause or irrelevant comorbidity.
- **Falsifier:** Reliable pre-exposure records showing the same symptoms and cross-setting impairment at comparable severity, with no temporal worsening after the proposed adult trigger.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Month-by-month onset timeline; mood, sleep, trauma, substance and medication chronology; work-demand changes; adherence; neurological/medical review; and collateral evidence from before and after the change.

### H2.1 — High academic or intellectual achievement is compatible with clinically meaningful impairment

- **Verdict:** Partially supported.
- **Certainty:** Very low.
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** In a 24-person adult ADHD study, participants in high, average, and below-average IQ strata had diagnosed ADHD and functional impairment and benefited to varying degrees from CBT (PMID 41089022).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** The study was retrospective, uncontrolled, and very small. It measured IQ rather than high achievement and did not report baseline impairment by IQ subgroup.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** High scores can coexist with efficient independent function, selective strengths, environmental privilege/support, or impairment arising from another condition.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** High IQ does not by itself exclude ADHD-related impairment, but achievement and impairment must both be measured; this sample does not quantify how often they coexist.
- **Falsifier:** Large controlled studies showing that high-IQ or high-achieving adults with validated ADHD have no clinically meaningful impairment after careful measurement.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Standardized IQ/achievement data, discrepancy between ability and output, missed deadlines/errors, effort and time cost, supports, recovery burden, and functioning outside academics.

### H2.2 — Higher intelligence can delay recognition of ADHD or autistic impairment

- **Verdict:** Indeterminate.
- **Certainty:** Very low.
- **Evidence directness:** Highly indirect.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** A systematic review of autism in women reported delayed diagnosis, social camouflaging, less externalizing presentation, and contextual moderators (PMID 42052784).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** Intelligence was not reported. In childhood ADHD, higher IQ was associated with a slightly lower probability of persistence rather than demonstrated diagnostic delay (PMID 40287009).
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Sex/gender bias, symptom presentation, access to care, family resources, school structure, comorbidity, and cultural expectations may delay recognition independently of intelligence.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Diagnostic delay and camouflaging occur in some autistic presentations, but this abstract sample does not establish higher intelligence as the cause of delayed ADHD or autism recognition.
- **Falsifier:** Prospective diagnostic-timing studies showing no association, or earlier rather than later recognition, with higher intelligence after controlling for sex, symptoms, impairment, access, and context.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** IQ and achievement records, age and reason for first concern/referral, prior missed assessments, sex/gender context, symptom visibility, support history, and contemporaneous impairment.

### H2.3 — Compensation can preserve achievement while increasing effort or instability

- **Verdict:** Indeterminate.
- **Certainty:** Very low.
- **Evidence directness:** Highly indirect.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Two neuroimaging studies within a review were interpreted as showing compensatory recruitment in older ADHD groups (PMID 42472451). A case-control biomarker study proposed a compensatory neuroprotective interpretation for NPTX2 (PMID 41489639). An autism review described camouflaging (PMID 42052784).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** None measured the full claim: preserved achievement plus compensatory strategies plus increased effort, cost, or instability. Neural compensation and social camouflaging are not interchangeable with academic or occupational compensation.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Achievement may reflect intact ability, strong interest, supportive structure, socioeconomic resources, selection effects, or efficient independent functioning.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Compensation is plausible, but it should be claimed only when preserved output is accompanied by directly measured strategies or supports and a demonstrable effort, recovery, or stability cost.
- **Falsifier:** High achievement with ordinary time/effort, stable performance under reduced structure, no unusual strategies or supports, and no rebound or recovery cost.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Time-on-task, sleep sacrificed, deadline dependence, external reminders, editing/rechecking, support use, variability under load, burnout/recovery, and comparison with peers of similar ability.

### H2.4 — Achievement alone does not establish compensation or masking

- **Verdict:** Partially supported.
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** The IQ-stratified adult ADHD study showed impairment can coexist with higher cognitive ability but did not measure compensation (PMID 41089022). The autism review required explicit camouflaging evidence rather than inferring it from achievement (PMID 42052784).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** No selected study directly tested whether observers misclassify achievement as compensation.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Achievement can arise from efficient functioning, selective strengths, extensive support, compensation, masking, favorable context, or combinations of these.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Achievement is not a valid proxy for compensation; compensation requires direct evidence of strategies, supports, discrepancy, or cost.
- **Falsifier:** A validated achievement threshold that accurately identifies compensation or masking across independent samples without direct strategy or cost measures.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Achievement trajectory, process used to obtain results, support intensity, error rates, time/effort, emotional and physical cost, and performance when structure is withdrawn.

### H2.N1 — Observed achievement may reflect efficient independent functioning rather than hidden impairment

- **Verdict:** Indeterminate.
- **Certainty:** Very low.
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Higher childhood IQ was associated with a slightly lower likelihood of adult ADHD persistence (PMID 40287009), which is compatible with but does not prove more efficient functioning.
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** Adults across IQ strata still had diagnosed ADHD and functional impairment in a very small treatment study (PMID 41089022). Neither study measured hidden effort or independent efficiency.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Efficient functioning, compensation, environmental support, selective impairment, regression to the mean, and diagnostic selection can all produce the same observed achievement.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Achievement may represent efficient functioning or hidden compensatory burden; outcome level alone cannot distinguish them.
- **Falsifier:** Direct process measures consistently showing that high achievers with ADHD require substantially more time, support, or recovery than matched peers despite equivalent output.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Objective task time, support dependence, performance variability, recovery cost, independent living skills, cross-setting impairment, and periods with reduced external structure.

### Overall Reviewer-1 conclusion

The abstract-level evidence is reasonably coherent for childhood-to-adult ADHD persistence and multi-domain impairment, with heterogeneity and meaningful alternative contributors. It is much weaker for the proposed intelligence → delayed recognition → compensation pathway. No personal diagnostic conclusion, causal attribution, or treatment recommendation is justified until full-text verification, independent Reviewer-2 adjudication, and clinical assessment are completed.

### H5.1 — ADHD is associated with social and peer difficulties

- **Verdict:** Supported.
- **Certainty:** Moderate.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct.
- **Directness to this case:** Low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** A three-wave study of 20,137 adolescents found that baseline ADHD symptoms predicted later outcomes through peer victimization and depression (PMID 41890162). Adult ADHD meta-analysis documented a modifiable social-function domain distinct from symptom change (PMID 41936228). A youth network meta-analysis also demonstrated measurable social dysfunction responsive to intervention (PMID 41932092).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** The large cohort used symptom scales rather than diagnosed ADHD. The exercise review was limited to ages 6-13 and tested intervention response, not baseline prevalence or causality.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Bullying, depression, anxiety, gaming behavior, family or school context, stigma, personality traits, and autistic social-communication features can independently contribute.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** ADHD symptoms and diagnoses are associated with measurable social and peer difficulty at group level, but the mechanism and cause of an individual's difficulty require direct social assessment and competing explanations.
- **Falsifier:** Large prospective studies with validated ADHD diagnoses showing no excess peer victimization, relationship difficulty, or social-function impairment versus matched controls.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Peer and relationship history, bullying exposure, social-cognition and communication measures, teacher/partner collateral, mood/anxiety screening, context, and symptom timing.

### H5.2 — Autistic features can explain additional social-communication variance beyond ADHD

- **Verdict:** Partially supported.
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** A very large autism meta-analysis found substantial social-function differences versus neurotypical controls across 22 components (Hedges g -0.744; PMID 42120904). Reviews recognize shared and distinct ADHD/autism features and the need for differential diagnosis (PMIDs 41579913 and 41388592).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** The large meta-analysis did not compare autism with ADHD. Only two studies in the EF/emotion-regulation review directly involved autism/ADHD co-occurrence, and clear between-condition differences could not be established.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Social anxiety, depression, personality pathology, language disorder, trauma, bullying, cultural norms, sensory load, and environmental mismatch may create similar social presentations.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Autism is robustly associated with social-communication differences, but this abstract sample does not quantify the incremental variance autistic features explain after ADHD and other causes are modeled.
- **Falsifier:** Well-powered comparative studies showing that validated autistic traits add no prediction of social-communication outcomes after ADHD severity, language, IQ, anxiety, trauma, and context are controlled.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Developmental social-communication history, restricted/repetitive patterns, sensory profile, pragmatic language testing, standardized autism and ADHD measures, informant reports, and multivariable differential assessment.

### H5.3 — Anxiety, trauma, mood state, substances, or conflict can explain social difficulties

- **Verdict:** Partially supported.
- **Certainty:** Moderate.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct for anxiety, depression, conflict, and personality features; absent or indirect for trauma and substances.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Social anxiety had a moderate, measure-dependent association with communication skills in autistic youth (PMID 41348918). Social motivation was moderately associated with anxiety, depression, and social anxiety across the autistic lifespan (PMID 41670017). Peer victimization and depression mediated part of the longitudinal ADHD association (PMID 41890162). Adult autism-personality differential evidence distinguished social-norm difficulty from conflict-linked emotional patterns (PMID 42319023).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** Most relationships were correlational or mediated and cannot establish that the alternative condition fully explains the social problem. Trauma and substance explanations were not directly tested in the selected abstracts.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** ADHD, autism, language differences, sensory overload, chronic exclusion, environmental mismatch, and developmental motor or cognitive differences may coexist with the psychiatric factor.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Anxiety, depression, conflict, bullying, and personality-related processes can contribute to or resemble neurodevelopmental social difficulty, but replacement of one explanation with another requires temporal and comparative evidence.
- **Falsifier:** Prospective studies showing social difficulties remain unchanged when the proposed alternative condition remits and are fully accounted for by stable neurodevelopmental measures.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Timeline of social change relative to anxiety, mood, trauma, substance use, conflict, bullying, medications, and environmental transitions; symptom remission data; collateral history.

### H5.4 — Multiple interacting conditions may explain social difficulties better than one diagnosis

- **Verdict:** Partially supported.
- **Certainty:** Moderate.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct to indirect.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** ADHD symptoms, peer victimization, and depression formed a temporally ordered pathway in a large cohort (PMID 41890162). Reviews found shared and distinct autism/ADHD features, executive/emotional cross-condition associations, and clinically important comorbidity (PMIDs 41579913 and 41388592). Internalizing symptoms covaried with autistic social motivation (PMID 41670017).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** No selected study directly compared the predictive accuracy of a multi-condition model with a fully specified single-diagnosis model for general social functioning.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** One primary condition plus environmental adversity, measurement overlap, referral bias, or common-method variance can appear as multimorbidity.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** A multi-factor formulation is often plausible and should be tested, but superiority over a single-condition explanation must be demonstrated with temporality, validated measures, and incremental prediction.
- **Falsifier:** Prospective models showing that one diagnosis alone explains social outcomes as well as or better than models adding comorbidity, context, and mediators.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Structured diagnostic assessments, symptom timelines, language/IQ data, trauma and mood screening, sensory profile, peer context, treatment-response history, and incremental functional prediction.

### H11.1 — ADHD and developmental coordination disorder commonly overlap

- **Verdict:** Indeterminate.
- **Certainty:** Very low.
- **Evidence directness:** Highly indirect.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** A meta-analysis found ADHD inattention related to weaker perceptual-motor functioning and hyperactivity-impulsivity related to a different motor-coordination pattern (PMID 41989410).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** The study assessed symptom dimensions, not diagnosed DCD, and reported no ADHD–DCD prevalence or co-occurrence estimate. None of the 14 selected abstracts directly tested this claim.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Motor difficulty in ADHD samples may reflect inattention during testing, autism, neurological disease, learning history, medication, obesity, injury, anxiety, or limited practice rather than DCD.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** ADHD can be associated with motor-performance differences, but this abstract sample cannot determine how commonly ADHD and diagnosed DCD overlap.
- **Falsifier:** High-quality diagnostic studies finding DCD prevalence in ADHD no higher than matched populations after standardized motor assessment and exclusions.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Formal DCD diagnostic history, childhood motor milestones, standardized motor testing, school/occupational motor impact, ADHD symptom control during testing, and neurological exclusion.

### H11.2 — Developmental coordination difficulties can persist into adulthood

- **Verdict:** Partially supported.
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Adults with a mean age of 45.6 years had measurable symptoms on an Adult DCD/Dyspraxia Checklist, and these symptoms related to sensory over-responsiveness and emotion regulation (PMID 42293966).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** Participants were community dwelling and may not have met DCD diagnostic thresholds. The study was cross-sectional and did not follow childhood DCD into adulthood.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Adult coordination complaints may reflect neurological disease, injury, medication, anxiety, sensory factors, low activity, pain, obesity, or limited practice.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Motor-coordination symptoms are measurable in midlife, but prospective childhood-to-adult evidence is required to establish persistence of diagnosed DCD.
- **Falsifier:** Longitudinal cohorts showing childhood DCD motor deficits routinely resolve by adulthood without continuing functional impact.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Childhood motor milestones and records, early DCD diagnosis, longitudinal motor performance, current standardized assessment, daily-life impact, and medical/neurological history.

### H11.3 — Motor impairment can exceed what inattention alone explains

- **Verdict:** Indeterminate.
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Autism meta-analysis found poorer balance than typical peers and multiple postural-sway abnormalities (PMID 41876864). A systematic review found frequent balance, dexterity, and coordination impairment in autistic youth (PMID 40941606). ADHD symptom dimensions had separable perceptual-motor and motor-coordination associations after mutual adjustment (PMID 41989410).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** Autism studies did not adjust for inattention or diagnose DCD. The ADHD meta-analysis did not compare ADHD with DCD and paradoxically linked hyperactivity-impulsivity with better motor coordination.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Task attention, motivation, comprehension, medication, anxiety, physical activity, sensory processing, obesity, injury, neurological disease, and practice can affect motor performance.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Motor impairment occurs in neurodevelopmental populations and may not track ADHD symptom dimensions uniformly, but the selected abstracts do not prove impairment beyond inattention in diagnosed DCD.
- **Falsifier:** Standardized studies showing motor deficits disappear after attention is experimentally controlled or ADHD symptoms are effectively treated.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Motor testing under controlled attention conditions, off/on-treatment comparison when safe, developmental motor history, occupational impact, physical activity, sensory profile, and neurological examination.

### H11.4 — Neurologic disease, injury, anxiety, medication, obesity, or limited practice can explain motor problems

- **Verdict:** Partially supported.
- **Certainty:** Very low.
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** In midlife adults, the association between sensory over-responsiveness and DCD symptom scores became non-significant after emotion-regulation difficulty was included, while the indirect effect remained significant (PMID 42293966). Motor estimates across autism studies varied markedly by measurement method and condition (PMIDs 41876864 and 40941606).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** The selected abstracts did not directly test neurological disease, injury, medication, obesity, or limited practice as alternative causes. Cross-sectional mediation cannot establish causality.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Persistent developmental DCD may coexist with acquired or contextual motor limitations rather than being replaced by them.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Adult motor complaints require systematic exclusion of acquired, medical, psychiatric, medication, activity, and practice-related explanations; the present abstract sample directly supports only limited sensory/emotional alternatives.
- **Falsifier:** Comprehensive prospective assessments showing these factors have no independent relationship with motor function after developmental DCD severity is measured.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Neurological examination, injury timeline, medication review, anxiety state, BMI and pain, vision/vestibular history, physical-activity and practice exposure, substance use, and standardized motor testing.

### Overall Reviewer-1 conclusion

The strongest evidence supports social and peer difficulties in ADHD and broad social-function differences in autism. It also supports an interacting formulation involving internalizing symptoms, bullying, conflict, and diagnostic overlap, but incremental autism-over-ADHD effects remain insufficiently quantified. The DCD claims are not ready for confident synthesis: direct ADHD–DCD prevalence, prospective adult persistence, and inattention-adjusted motor studies are missing from this bounded abstract sample. No personal diagnostic conclusion or treatment recommendation is justified until full-text verification, independent Reviewer-2 adjudication, and clinical assessment are completed.

PMID 32954909 did not report coaching-specific effects, and PMID 36009086 found insufficient evidence for school-based and executive interventions when generating guideline recommendations for core symptoms.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Nonspecific therapist contact, expectancy, medication changes, concurrent supports, practice, or self-report bias may contribute. Directly targeting a domain may improve that domain without generalizing to quality of life.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Adult ADHD CBT that directly targets everyday functioning has moderate short-term evidence, and organizational-skills programs help many young people; durable functional benefit from ADHD coaching as a distinct intervention remains uncertain.
- **Falsifier:** Adequately powered trials with active attention controls showing no objective or blinded functional benefit, no maintenance, and no domain-specific advantage for CBT or coaching.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Treatment content and fidelity, provider qualification, medication and concurrent care, prespecified functional targets, blinded or objective outcomes, attendance, homework completion, burden, adverse effects, and follow-up beyond 3 months.

#### H4.3 — Digital aids or assistive technology support executive functioning

- **Verdict:** Partially supported.
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct for technology interventions, but often indirect for everyday executive functioning.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** PMID 37988139 found small improvements in computer-rated inattention and parent-rated executive function (both SMD = -0.35), behavior, visual attention, and reaction time across 19 pediatric randomized trials. PMID 40522458 found improvements in visuospatial short-term memory (MD = 0.67) and working memory (MD = 0.47) across 8 randomized game trials.
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** Behavioral inhibition and monitoring were nonsignificant in PMID 40522458. The umbrella review PMID 40264083 judged effectiveness inconclusive because underlying evidence was generally low quality and found inconsistently reported adverse effects. A post-hoc, hypothesis-generating mixed-population review outside the frozen target-population eligibility criteria found far transfer and long-term effects limited (PMID 42199316); PMID 41808914 identified major adult implementation and integration gaps. PMID 42199316 does not contribute to the verdict.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Practice effects, expectancy, parent-report changes, gaming engagement, novelty, or improvement on tasks resembling the training may account for gains without durable real-world support.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Some digital interventions yield small improvements in trained or closely related cognitive and behavioral measures, but durable transfer to everyday organization and functioning is not established.
- **Falsifier:** Independent long-term randomized trials with active digital controls showing no objective functional improvement, no far transfer, or rapid loss of benefit after training stops.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Exact tool and target, usage logs, adherence, real-world completion and error outcomes, near- and far-transfer measures, screen burden, sleep, distress, problematic use, accessibility, cost, privacy, and post-withdrawal maintenance.

#### H4.4 — Occupational or educational redesign reduces impairment

- **Verdict:** Partially supported.
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct for school-based intervention packages, indirect for structural redesign itself.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** PMID 40761448 found small improvements from school-based randomized trials in academic performance (d = 0.37), social skills (d = 0.28), inattention (d = -0.33), and externalizing problems (d = -0.32). PMID 28088557 found organizational and academic gains, while adult qualitative synthesis PMID 28398986 described clearer work structure, rules, limits, and coaching as protective.
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** School interventions bundled multiple components, hyperactivity or impulsivity did not significantly improve in PMID 40761448, and heterogeneity and reporter bias were concerns. PMID 36009086 found insufficient data for guideline recommendations on school-based and executive interventions for core symptoms.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Skills training, teacher or caregiver attention, contingency management, treatment adherence, or selection of motivated schools and participants may explain effects attributed to redesign.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Multicomponent school supports can produce small average functional gains for young people with ADHD; the causal effect and durability of a specific educational or workplace redesign remain uncertain.
- **Falsifier:** Cluster-randomized or stepped-wedge redesign studies showing no improvement in objective attendance, completion, errors, academic or work output, safety, or wellbeing after controlling co-interventions.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Specific environmental change, baseline demand and impairment, objective work or school outcomes, attendance, errors, task completion, social function, co-interventions, implementation fidelity, staff burden, and maintenance across terms or job cycles.

#### H4.5 — AI-assisted organization has durable clinical benefit

- **Verdict:** Indeterminate.
- **Certainty:** Very low.
- **Evidence directness:** No direct durable clinical evidence in the selected set.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** PMID 41808914 mapped machine-learning and other digital technologies for adult ADHD clinical management, but did not report AI organization outcomes. No selected record evaluated an AI organizer with clinically meaningful functional follow-up.
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** The adult digital-health literature was described as fragmented and preliminary, with unresolved integration, adherence, lived-experience, implementation, and value questions. Digital reviews more broadly found limited far transfer and long-term evidence.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Any apparent benefit may come from ordinary reminders, external accountability, novelty, increased attention to goals, concurrent treatment, or selective continued use rather than AI-specific capability.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** There is insufficient evidence to claim that AI-assisted organization produces durable clinical or functional benefit in ADHD or executive dysfunction.
- **Falsifier:** Independent, preregistered trials comparing an AI organizer with an equivalent non-AI scaffold and active control, demonstrating durable objective functional benefit, acceptable burden and safety, and maintenance after support reduction.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Defined clinical and functional target, AI versus non-AI comparator, usage and override logs, errors and hallucinations, privacy exposure, workload shifted to the user or caregiver, adverse events, dependency, costs, objective outcomes, and at least medium-term follow-up.

#### H4.6 — External scaffolds may have limited durability, dependence, burden, or harms

- **Verdict:** Supported.
- **Certainty:** Moderate.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct for digital safety, transfer, and follow-up limitations; indirect for long-term dependence on nondigital supports.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** PMID 40264083 found low-quality and inconclusive digital evidence, with adverse effects reported in 8 of 26 reviews and inconsistent harms ascertainment. PMID 40522458 found cognitive gains without significant behavioral executive-function change; PMID 41808914 identified adherence and implementation gaps; PMID 41936228 noted short follow-up and nonsignificant follow-up quality-of-life effects.
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** Limitations are not universal. Adult CBT global-function effects persisted or increased at short follow-up in PMID 41936228, and organizational or school interventions produced measurable functional gains in PMIDs 28088557 and 40761448.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Weak durability or transfer may reflect immature tools, poor task matching, inadequate training dose, insensitive outcome measurement, or implementation failure rather than an inherent limit of external scaffolding.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** External scaffolds can help selected outcomes, but benefit is often domain-specific and incompletely tested for durability; burden, dependence, inconsistent use, poor transfer, and harms should be measured rather than assumed absent.
- **Falsifier:** Large independent trials showing broad objective functional benefit maintained after support tapering, with low burden, low adverse-event rates, minimal caregiver transfer of workload, and replication across settings.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Benefit and burden over time, support dose, missed-use consequences, independent functioning during planned taper, caregiver or staff workload, adverse effects, problematic use, sleep, privacy, costs, satisfaction, and objective cross-setting transfer.

### Reviewer handoff

Every extracted record requires full-text checking for intervention components, diagnosis and age separability, comparator details, risk of bias, exact functional outcomes, harms ascertainment, and follow-up duration. Human Reviewer 2 should independently adjudicate eligibility, extraction, and each claim verdict. Clinical sign-off should additionally assess contraindications, privacy, risk of dependency, caregiver or staff burden, and whether any individualized trial is safe and clinically appropriate.

## Sleep and Circadian Modifiers — Module Synthesis

**AI Reviewer-1 draft. Human Reviewer 2 and clinical sign-off pending. Abstract-only evidence; not diagnostic or treatment guidance.**

### Evidence set and claim ceiling

Fourteen screened include/uncertain records were selected to cover all H7 claims across meta-analytic, prospective, objective, repeated-measures, genetic, and null-evidence designs. The synthesis can support calibrated group-level associations and competing temporal explanations. It cannot establish an individual's diagnosis, causation, or response to changing sleep.

### H7.1 — Short or inadequate sleep can worsen attention and executive functioning

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct for association; indirect for causation.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** In a large prospective adolescent cohort, shorter wearable-derived sleep predicted later attention symptoms, although effects were small (PMID 41870441). In adults with ADHD, actigraphy-linked latency and duration correlated with selective attention and inhibition (PMID 41803808).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** Most adult-ADHD correlations did not survive multiple-comparison correction (PMID 41803808). In euthymic bipolar disorder, longer duration as well as poorer continuity related to worse cognitive outcomes, arguing against a simple linear short-sleep model (PMID 42371207).
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Baseline ADHD symptoms, emotion dysregulation, cognitive disengagement, mood state, medication, or sleep-disordered breathing may produce both sleep disturbance and cognitive difficulty (PMIDs 41618215, 42522250).
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Shorter or disrupted sleep is associated with worse attention or executive outcomes in several adolescent and clinical samples, but abstract-level evidence does not establish that short sleep causes within-person deterioration in ADHD or bipolar disorder.
- **Falsifier:** A sufficiently powered preregistered within-person sleep-extension or sleep-restriction study in diagnosed ADHD, using objective sleep and blinded cognitive testing, finds no cognitive change after controlling mood, medication, circadian timing, and practice effects.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Repeated objective sleep duration and continuity, daily medication and substance timing, mood state, shift schedule, OSA risk, and repeated objective attention or executive testing during comparable task conditions.

### H7.2 — Sleep quality and circadian irregularity can worsen ADHD-related functioning

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct for cross-sectional clinical association; indirect for temporal effect.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Adults with ADHD showed more delayed and unstable rhythms than controls and some sleep-cognition associations (PMID 41803808). Eveningness, poorer sleep, and cognitive disengagement formed a significant serial pathway to self-reported ADHD dimensions in treatment-seeking adults (PMID 42522250).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** The adult ADHD actigraphy findings were multiplicity-sensitive, and the serial-mediation sample did not have confirmed ADHD and was cross-sectional (PMIDs 41803808, 42522250).
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Cognitive disengagement, mood symptoms, experiential avoidance, or ADHD severity may worsen sleep rather than sleep worsening ADHD functioning (PMIDs 42522250, 41618215).
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Poor sleep quality and irregular or delayed rhythms correlate with ADHD-related symptoms and selected cognitive outcomes, but direction, specificity, and real-world functional impact remain uncertain.
- **Falsifier:** Longitudinal objective monitoring in diagnosed ADHD shows that sleep-quality and rhythm changes do not precede symptom or function changes and that only prior ADHD severity predicts later sleep.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Confirmed diagnostic status, actigraphic regularity and timing, validated sleep quality, daily ADHD symptoms, objective work or school function, cognitive disengagement, mood, and medication timing.

### H7.3 — Shift work can impair sleep, cognition, or mood

- **Verdict:** Indeterminate
- **Certainty:** Very low.
- **Evidence directness:** Highly indirect.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** A narrative review of abrupt daylight-saving-time disruption reports impaired attention, executive function, emotional stability, and manic risk in vulnerable populations that include ADHD and bipolar disorder (PMID 42192834).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** The selected set contains no direct shift-work cohort with separable ADHD or bipolar outcomes. DST is a short, population-wide clock change, not chronic or rotating shift work.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Workload, occupational stress, light exposure, social jetlag, chronotype selection, and baseline illness severity may account for apparent shift-work effects.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Abrupt circadian clock shifts are associated with short-term cognitive and mood disruption at a population level; this sample does not establish the magnitude or specificity of shift-work effects in ADHD or bipolar disorder.
- **Falsifier:** A prospective matched shift-work study with objective sleep and circadian measurement finds no within-person cognitive or mood deterioration after adjustment for workload, chronotype, medication, and baseline severity.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Exact rotating or night-shift schedule, light exposure, chronotype, days since schedule transition, objective sleep timing, workload, baseline symptoms, medication, and repeated cognition or mood measures.

### H7.4 — Within-person symptom variation can track sleep changes

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct.
- **Directness to this case:** Low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Six months of daily bipolar data showed that recent sleep variation tracked mood instability, including a non-linear duration relationship (PMID 42320790). A medRxiv preprint using prospective actigraphy and imaging also linked rhythm instability with later mania symptoms in at-risk youth (PMID 41646692).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** In ten-week objective monitoring, mood instability appeared unrelated to circadian indices despite group-level rhythm differences (PMID 42464599). The daily bipolar study explicitly could not establish temporal ordering (PMID 42320790).
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Emerging mood changes may disrupt sleep, or stress, medication, substance use, and activity may shift both simultaneously.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Sleep variability and mood instability can co-vary within people with bipolar disorder, but lagged causal direction and generalization to ADHD are not established.
- **Falsifier:** High-frequency objective sleep plus repeated symptom sampling shows no lagged sleep-to-symptom association after autoregressive control, while symptom-to-sleep effects or shared exposures explain the observed covariance.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Daily mood, irritability, attention, and energy ratings; actigraphy; sleep timing and duration; medications; substances; stressors; menstrual or postpartum state where relevant; and enough observations for bidirectional lagged models.

### H7.5 — Sleep loss can precipitate or amplify bipolar-spectrum mood symptoms

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct for some rhythm-to-mania associations; indirect for a simple sleep-loss causal claim.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** A medRxiv preprint linked rest-activity instability prospectively through white-matter measures to later mania symptoms in vulnerable youth (PMID 41646692). A very large matched cohort found OSA preceded higher bipolar diagnosis risk (PMID 41262297).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** A medRxiv preprint found that longer rather than shorter sleep predicted combined bipolar-or-psychotic transition (PMID 41822684). Genetic liability to insomnia or short sleep was inversely associated with postpartum psychosis, and Mendelian randomization did not support a causal sleep-trait effect (PMID 41644956). Phase-based sleep differences may reflect symptoms of the mood state (PMID 40706099).
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Sleep change may be a prodrome, consequence, or marker of shared circadian, inflammatory, medication, postpartum, or illness-severity processes rather than a sufficient trigger.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Sleep and rest-activity disturbances are clinically relevant markers of bipolar vulnerability and mood state, but this abstract set does not establish a universal, linear causal rule that short sleep precipitates mania.
- **Falsifier:** Prospective within-person studies show that objectively shorter sleep never precedes manic change after prodromal symptoms are modeled, or that the association disappears after medication, stress, and activity adjustment.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Confirmed bipolar subtype and episode chronology, euthymic baseline, daily mania and depression ratings, objective sleep and activity, medication and substance timing, postpartum status, OSA evaluation, and documented lag between sleep and symptom change.

### H7.N1 — Apparent sleep effects may reflect reverse causation from mood or baseline severity

- **Verdict:** Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct for reverse-pathway plausibility; causal partition remains incomplete.
- **Directness to this case:** Low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Sleep characteristics differed by bipolar mood phase and persisted in euthymia (PMID 40706099). ADHD symptoms and emotional processes statistically predicted insomnia in an explicitly reverse-oriented model (PMID 41618215). Genetic analysis did not support a simple causal sleep effect on postpartum psychosis (PMID 41644956).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** Mendelian-randomization synthesis linked sleep traits to ADHD and bipolar risk (PMID 41517697), and OSA preceded incident bipolar diagnosis in a matched cohort (PMID 41262297), so reverse causation is unlikely to be the only pathway.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Bidirectional feedback or shared causes—stress, medication, substances, inflammation, chronotype, or emerging illness—may fit better than either one-way model.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Reverse and bidirectional causation are credible and must be modeled; sleep-symptom association alone should not be interpreted as sleep causing neurodevelopmental or bipolar symptoms.
- **Falsifier:** Multiple objective, temporally dense studies consistently show sleep changes precede later symptoms while prior symptoms do not predict later sleep, after shared confounders and prodromal changes are controlled.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** A temporally ordered symptom and sleep record spanning stable and unstable periods, with medication, substance, workload, stress, and medical sleep-disorder data sufficient for bidirectional modeling.

### Overall module conclusion

The strongest defensible conclusion is a **bidirectional modifier model**: sleep and circadian measures often correlate with attention, cognition, and bipolar mood instability, but effects vary by phenotype, exposure, and time scale. Objective longitudinal evidence exists, yet simple causal claims—especially shift-work specificity and universal short-sleep-triggered mania—remain above the current evidence ceiling.

## Module E2 — Mood, substances, and medication state effects

**Status:** AI Reviewer‑1 evidence synthesis draft. Human Reviewer 2 and treating-clinician sign-off are pending. This is not a diagnosis or treatment recommendation.

### Scope and interpretation

This module separates three questions that should not be collapsed: whether an exposure can change cognition or behavior acutely; whether it changes population-level risk of a syndromal episode; and whether it caused any episode in one person. The reviewed literature supports the first two for several exposures but cannot answer the third without a dated longitudinal clinical record.

### Claim verdicts

#### H8.1 — Mood episodes can produce episodic executive, speech, salience, and impulse-control changes

- **Verdict:** Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate.
- **Directness to this case:** Low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Current NICE bipolar guidance treats mania/hypomania as episodic states requiring specialist assessment and specifically requires consideration of ADHD, drug misuse, alcohol-use disorders, and physical causes in the differential. The qualitative systematic review in PMID 42316899 shows that hypomania/mania can be accompanied by perceived vitality, ability, achievement, and creativity, which can alter self-appraisal.
- **Counterevidence/qualification:** Executive difficulty, rapid speech, confidence, or salience is not specific to bipolar disorder. The literature does not determine whether any reported feature occurred inside a syndromal mood episode.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Lifelong neurodevelopmental traits, anxiety, sleep loss, substances, medication, or ordinary meaning-making.
- **Calibrated claim:** Bipolar-spectrum episodes can cause time-limited changes in energy, speech, impulse control, judgment, and perceived significance; temporal clustering with other episode criteria is required before applying that explanation to an individual.
- **Falsifier:** Stable intensity across sustained euthymia with adequate sleep and abstinence would weaken an exclusively episodic account.
- **Useful personal/clinical data:** Prospective mood/sleep/energy charting, medication and substance timeline, collateral observations, episode duration, functional consequences, and structured bipolar assessment.

#### H8.2 — Cannabis or THC can worsen cognition, anxiety, psychosis risk, or mood stability

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate.
- **Directness to this case:** Low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** PMID 37648266 found convincing observational evidence for psychosis (OR 1.71, 95% CI 1.47–2.00) and converging psychiatric/cognitive harms; PMID 38655516 linked cannabis exposure with manic symptoms and poorer bipolar prognosis. Potency and dose-response evidence further argues against treating all cannabis exposure as equivalent.
- **Counterevidence/qualification:** Most long-term risk evidence is observational and confounded. PMID 41916924 found very little evidence that ADHD specifically magnifies cannabis-associated psychosis risk. Recovery after cessation is variable and often substantial (PMID 41872072).
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Reverse causation, shared liability, other substances, baseline illness severity, sleep disruption, or exposure misclassification.
- **Calibrated claim:** Cannabis—especially frequent or high-THC exposure—is associated with psychosis and adverse mood/cognitive outcomes; causal attribution and magnitude for one person require temporal and dose information.
- **Falsifier:** Repeated, well-documented exposure without temporal change would weaken a strong individual causal claim but would not erase population-level risk.
- **Useful personal/clinical data:** Product THC/CBD content, route, dose, frequency, timing relative to sleep and symptoms, abstinence periods, toxicology when clinically indicated, and collateral observations.

#### H8.3 — Prescription stimulants can produce state-dependent activation or mood effects

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate.
- **Directness to this case:** Low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** PMID 39262211 found higher adjusted odds of incident psychosis/mania with prescription amphetamine and a dose-response signal; PMID 40900605 found higher psychosis occurrence with amphetamines than methylphenidate.
- **Counterevidence/qualification:** In mood-stabilized bipolar disorder, systematic reviews and a large within-subject cohort did not find higher mania outcomes (PMIDs 38433530, 38670627, 41856665). High heterogeneity and confounding prevent a single class-wide causal estimate.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Underlying bipolar illness, indication/severity, sleep loss, nonadherence to mood stabilization, misuse, dose, or co-use of nicotine/cannabis.
- **Calibrated claim:** Prescribed stimulants carry an uncommon but clinically important activation/psychosis risk that appears sensitive to agent, dose, vulnerability, and mood stabilization; risk is not inevitable.
- **Falsifier:** Consistent absence of activation across supervised therapeutic exposures during stable sleep and mood would weaken an individual susceptibility hypothesis.
- **Useful personal/clinical data:** Exact agent and dose, formulation, adherence, titration dates, mood stabilizer exposure, sleep, co-exposures, clinician observations, and symptom resolution after dose change.

#### H8.4 — Nicotine can produce acute activation and dependence-related state changes

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Directness to this case:** Low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** PMID 37216808 reports behavioral and neuropharmacologic interaction between nicotine and prescription stimulants; PMID 40050542 describes dose-dependent acute attention effects.
- **Counterevidence/qualification:** Evidence specific to bipolar activation is sparse; much interaction evidence is preclinical or narrative. Acute attentional change is not durable clinical benefit and must be separated from withdrawal relief and dependence.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Withdrawal reversal, stimulant co-use, expectancy, sleep, or baseline attentional variability.
- **Calibrated claim:** Nicotine can acutely change arousal and attention and has dependence/withdrawal effects; evidence that it independently causes bipolar-spectrum activation is insufficient.
- **Falsifier:** No within-person change across carefully observed exposure/withdrawal periods would weaken a strong state-effect hypothesis.
- **Useful personal/clinical data:** Nicotine dose/formulation, timing, withdrawal intervals, stimulant/caffeine co-use, heart rate, sleep, and contemporaneous mood ratings.

#### H8.5 — Medication effects can mimic or amplify baseline neurodevelopmental traits

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate.
- **Directness to this case:** Low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Stimulants increase overall adverse events in randomized trials (PMID 40343695), and agent/dose-specific psychosis/mania associations appear in large observational evidence (PMIDs 39262211, 40900605).
- **Counterevidence/qualification:** Mood-stabilized bipolar cohorts do not show inevitable mania risk and sometimes show better hospitalization outcomes during ADHD treatment (PMID 41856665).
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Natural symptom fluctuation, underlying mood episode, nocebo/expectancy, or concurrent sleep/substance changes.
- **Calibrated claim:** Medication can alter arousal, sleep, speech, impulse control, or cognition, but attributing a change requires a reproducible temporal relation, dechallenge/rechallenge evidence when clinically safe, and control of co-occurring state changes.
- **Falsifier:** Persistence unchanged across clinician-supervised medication changes would weaken medication causation.
- **Useful personal/clinical data:** Pharmacy record, dose-by-date timeline, adherence, adverse-event ratings, co-medications, sleep, and clinician-observed changes.

#### H8.6 — Sleep, mood, cannabis, stimulants, and nicotine can interact

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Directness to this case:** Low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Nicotine–prescription-stimulant interaction is directly reviewed in PMID 37216808; cannabis and mood-course associations are documented in PMID 38655516. Adjacent E1 evidence supports sleep as a mood/executive modifier.
- **Counterevidence/qualification:** Direct factorial human studies of all modifiers together are lacking; apparent interaction can arise from correlated exposures, common vulnerability, or reverse causation. PMID 41916924 does not show an ADHD-specific multiplication of cannabis psychosis risk.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Additive independent effects, confounding by severity, or a primary episode leading to greater substance use and less sleep.
- **Calibrated claim:** Pairwise interactions are plausible and sometimes supported, but a unified multi-exposure interaction for this individual remains a hypothesis requiring prospective temporal data.
- **Falsifier:** Stable symptoms despite meaningful isolated changes in each exposure would weaken the proposed interaction.
- **Useful personal/clinical data:** Daily time-stamped multivariable charting with one clinically supervised change at a time; intentional sleep deprivation or substance rechallenge is not appropriate.

#### H8.7 — Stable persistence during euthymia, abstinence, and adequate sleep supports a trait-level component

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Directness to this case:** Low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Diagnostic logic and the reviewed cessation/within-subject literature support repeated assessment outside acute states. PMID 41872072 shows that some cannabis-associated cognitive changes recover while some persist beyond acute abstinence.
- **Counterevidence/qualification:** Residual symptoms can reflect slow recovery, medication effects, chronic mood burden, anxiety, or measurement noise; persistence alone does not identify ADHD, autism, DCD, or another trait.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Residual mood symptoms, chronic sleep disorder, long-term substance effects, or other medical/psychiatric causes.
- **Calibrated claim:** Demonstrating the same impairment across sustained euthymia, adequate sleep, and abstinence would support—but not prove—a trait component and should be combined with childhood-onset and cross-setting evidence.
- **Falsifier:** Full and durable resolution when state modifiers normalize would weaken a stable-trait account.
- **Useful personal/clinical data:** Defined observation windows, objective/subjective sleep measures, collateral function ratings, standardized ADHD/autism assessment, and verified exposure timelines.

### Module-level conclusion

The evidence most strongly supports treating mood episodes, cannabis/THC, stimulant agent and dose, nicotine, and sleep as separable time-varying variables. It does **not** support either extreme conclusion that all reported changes are neurodevelopmental traits or that prescribed stimulants invariably cause mania. The clinically discriminating evidence is longitudinal: symptom onset, duration, cross-setting childhood history, sleep, dose, co-exposures, and persistence during sustained euthymia and abstinence.

## Maladaptive Daydreaming, Rejection, Anxiety, and Compensation — Module Synthesis

**AI Reviewer-1 draft. Human Reviewer 2 and clinical sign-off pending. Abstract-only evidence; not diagnostic or treatment guidance.**

### Evidence set and claim ceiling

Twelve screened include/uncertain records were selected to cover every H9 and H12 claim. The set includes focal maladaptive-daydreaming evidence, longitudinal null evidence, meta-analytic alternative constructs, systematic reviews of bullying and social anxiety, and two longitudinal mediation studies. Only two selected records directly measured maladaptive daydreaming; that scarcity sharply limits H9.2-H9.4 conclusions.

### H9.1 — Maladaptive or immersive daydreaming is associated with loneliness, rejection, shame, or boredom

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Very low.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct.
- **Directness to this case:** Low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** A 40-study meta-analysis found operationalized maladaptive daydreaming positively associated with loneliness and shame, along with broad distress and psychopathology (PMID 39999955).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** The abstract provides no pooled construct-specific estimates and does not report rejection- or boredom-specific results. Association does not establish which construct preceded the other.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Depression, anxiety, dissociation, trauma, compulsivity, cognitive disengagement, or shared self-report bias could generate the observed association.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Maladaptive daydreaming is associated with loneliness, shame, emotion-regulation difficulty, and broad psychological distress; this sample does not establish specific rejection or boredom effects or temporal direction.
- **Falsifier:** Replicated longitudinal studies using validated MD measures find no association with loneliness or shame after controlling depression, dissociation, trauma, and compulsivity.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** A validated MD measure, functional-impairment threshold, episode timing, contemporaneous loneliness, shame, boredom and rejection ratings, mood, dissociation, trauma, and compulsive-behavior measures.

### H9.2 — Daydreaming can function as emotional compensation or escape

- **Verdict:** Indeterminate
- **Certainty:** Very low.
- **Evidence directness:** Highly indirect.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** The MD meta-analysis found associations with emotion-regulation difficulty and distress, which is compatible with—but does not test—an escape or compensation function (PMID 39999955).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** No selected abstract reports qualitative motives, immediate relief, experimental affect regulation, or mediation by escape. In a two-wave study, no positive MD cross-lagged effect was reported (PMID 40932793).
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** MD may reflect compulsive habit, absorption, dissociation, trauma-related coping, cognitive disengagement, normative fantasy, or an epiphenomenon of mood symptoms.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Emotional compensation is a plausible hypothesis for some people, not an established general function of maladaptive or immersive daydreaming.
- **Falsifier:** Ecological-momentary or experimental studies show that distress does not precede MD episodes, that episodes do not reduce distress even briefly, and that reported escape motives do not predict episode onset or persistence.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Before-during-after episode affect, triggers, stated motives, immediate and delayed relief, controllability, duration, impairment, competing activities, and measures of dissociation, compulsivity, trauma, and normative fantasy.

### H9.3 — Temporal direction between adversity and daydreaming is established

- **Verdict:** Unsupported
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct.
- **Directness to this case:** Low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** No selected abstract positively establishes adversity-to-MD or MD-to-adversity direction.
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** A two-wave cross-lagged study measured MD-related and dissociative constructs but found only problematic social-media use predicting later bodily dissociation and absorption; no other cross-lagged effects were detected (PMID 40932793). The focal meta-analysis is associational (PMID 39999955).
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Effects may be bidirectional, person-specific, too short-lived for sparse waves, or driven by shared mood, trauma, dissociation, or digital-behavior factors.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Temporal direction between adversity and maladaptive daydreaming is not established in this abstract set.
- **Falsifier:** Multiple preregistered longitudinal studies with at least three waves and validated MD measures consistently demonstrate the same cross-lagged direction after baseline symptoms and shared causes are controlled.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Repeated prospective measurement of adversity and MD, episode-level time stamps, baseline trait levels, and competing mood, trauma, dissociation, compulsivity, and social-media exposures.

### H9.4 — Normative fantasy, dissociation, compulsivity, trauma, or mood can better explain daydreaming

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** MD is strongly comorbid with dissociation, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, trauma, depression, and anxiety (PMID 39999955). Cognitive disengagement syndrome is a distinct daydreaming and hypoarousal-related construct with robust internalizing associations after ADHD adjustment (PMID 42341946).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** Neither record performs a comprehensive head-to-head explanatory model showing that alternatives account for MD's defining phenomenology or impairment. The focal meta-analysis treats MD as not fully explained by existing psychopathology (PMID 39999955).
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** MD may have incremental validity as a distinct construct while still sharing mechanisms and symptoms with dissociation, compulsivity, trauma, mood, or CDS.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Alternative constructs are necessary differential explanations and may account for portions of MD-like experience, but this sample does not show that they fully or better explain operationalized maladaptive daydreaming.
- **Falsifier:** Well-validated longitudinal models show that MD has stable incremental prediction of impairment and a distinct factor structure after comprehensive control for normative fantasy, CDS, dissociation, OCD symptoms, trauma, and mood.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Detailed phenomenology and impairment plus validated measures of MD, normative fantasy, CDS, dissociation, compulsivity or OCD, trauma, mood, psychosis, substance use, and sleep.

### H12.1 — Anxiety, shame, rejection, or bullying are associated with functional burden

- **Verdict:** Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct.
- **Directness to this case:** Low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** A 74-study autism review linked bullying to depression, anxiety, and social withdrawal (PMID 41425822). Adult ADHD stigma was linked to impairment, self-esteem, quality of life, and care behavior (PMID 42137527). Shame, fear, and alienation appraisals tracked trauma burden in autistic adults (PMID 41454656).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** Effect estimates and prospective design breakdowns are often absent; some associations vary by measure, cognition, and developmental stage (PMID 41348918).
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Core communication differences, emotion-regulation difficulty, baseline mood, family mental health, socioeconomic context, and ascertainment can influence both exposure and burden.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Anxiety, shame, stigma, victimization, and bullying are consistently associated with psychological and functional burden in neurodevelopmental populations, but association alone does not identify individual causation.
- **Falsifier:** Well-controlled prospective studies repeatedly find no subsequent functional or mental-health burden after measured bullying, rejection, shame, or anxiety across ADHD and autistic groups.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Standardized exposure history, timing and persistence, anxiety and shame measures, social and occupational or school function, baseline symptoms, communication profile, family context, and protective supports.

### H12.2 — Anxiety or rejection-related distress mediates avoidance or social dysfunction

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct for specific modeled outcomes; indirect for the broad claim.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Fear of negative evaluation, safety behaviors, and peer victimization were associated with social-anxiety maintenance in autism (PMID 39562437). A three-wave study supported a serial ADHD-to-bullying-to-depression-to-gaming-disorder pathway (PMID 41890162). Shame, fear, and alienation statistically mediated trauma burden in autistic adults (PMID 41454656).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** All studies in the social-anxiety review were cross-sectional. In a large longitudinal ADHD study, peer problems were tested but only self-esteem and parental mental health emerged as significant candidate mediators (PMID 41789525).
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Self-esteem, parental mental health, emotion-regulation difficulty, communication demands, stigma, or baseline psychopathology may mediate the same outcomes.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Rejection-related and anxious distress mediate some specific outcomes in some populations, but they are not established as universal or exclusive mediators of avoidance or social dysfunction.
- **Falsifier:** Prospective models with temporal separation and competing mediators consistently show no indirect effect through anxiety, shame, bullying, or rejection once self-esteem, family mental health, and baseline function are controlled.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Temporally separated exposure, mediator, and outcome measures; avoidance and social-function endpoints; self-esteem; family mental health; communication demands; emotion regulation; and baseline symptoms.

### H12.3 — Prospective evidence establishes the direction of this mediation

- **Verdict:** Unsupported
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct for one specific pathway; indirect for generalization.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** The three-wave ADHD study temporally ordered bullying and depressive symptoms before later gaming-disorder severity and used alternative-model sensitivity analyses (PMID 41890162).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** The supported bullying pathway was restricted by sex and developmental stage and had a narrow gaming outcome. A different large longitudinal mediation study prioritized self-esteem and parental mental health rather than peer problems (PMID 41789525). The autism social-anxiety evidence was entirely cross-sectional (PMID 39562437), and camouflaging evidence appeared bidirectional (PMID 42311563).
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Different pathways may dominate by age, sex, phenotype, outcome, and context; unmeasured confounding can remain even with longitudinal mediation.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Prospective support exists for a specific ADHD-bullying-depression-gaming pathway, but directional mediation across rejection, anxiety, avoidance, and social dysfunction is not established generally.
- **Falsifier:** Multiple preregistered cohorts across ADHD and autism reproduce the same temporally ordered indirect pathway across diverse functional outcomes, with negative-control and competing-mediator analyses.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** At least three temporally separated waves, exposure onset, mediator change, later function, baseline autoregressive controls, competing mediators, and developmental or sex-specific analyses.

### H12.4 — Functional difficulty can remain independently associated with neurodevelopmental traits

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Evidence directness:** Direct for statistical independence; indirect for trait causation.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** ADHD and autistic adolescent groups showed higher school emotional burden, while emotional burden and emotion-regulation difficulty independently predicted anxiety or depression (PMID 40707015). Adult ADHD symptoms correlated with internalized stigma and functional burden (PMID 42137527).
- **Strongest counterevidence or qualification:** The school study was cross-sectional, used symptom cutoffs, and did not establish residual trait effects after all contextual mediators. Camouflaging-mental-health relationships also appeared in non-autistic people (PMID 42311563), and communication-anxiety effects varied substantially (PMID 41348918).
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Contextual emotional burden, stigma, communication mismatch, CDS, self-esteem, family mental health, or mood may explain additional variance beyond diagnostic traits.
- **Corrected calibrated claim:** Neurodevelopmental traits and contextual or emotional factors can each retain statistical associations in some models, but their independent causal contributions to an individual's functional burden remain unresolved.
- **Falsifier:** Longitudinal models show that neurodevelopmental traits have no residual association with later function after contextual burden, stigma, anxiety, communication, self-esteem, family mental health, and baseline function are measured well.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Confirmed diagnosis and dimensional traits, repeated real-world function, context-specific burden, communication demands, stigma, anxiety, self-esteem, family mental health, mood, and developmental history.

### Overall module conclusion

The evidence is strongest for **association between social adversity or internalized distress and burden**. It is weaker for universal mediation and weak-to-absent for the emotional-compensation and temporal-direction claims about maladaptive daydreaming. The calibrated model should keep MD, dissociation, CDS, mood, compulsivity, trauma, stigma, and environmental mismatch as competing or interacting explanations rather than collapsing them into one causal story.

## Global model and null-hypothesis synthesis

**Status:** AI Reviewer‑1 evidence synthesis draft. Human Reviewer 2 and treating-clinician sign-off are pending.

#### G1 — Baseline traits and state or environmental modifiers interact

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Separate evidence streams document prospectively persistent childhood ADHD and adult functional burden (PMID 42365682), context- and reward-sensitive task processes (PMID 40287643), and within-person associations between recent sleep variation and mood instability in bipolar disorder (PMID 42320790).
- **Strongest opposing evidence or qualification:** No selected longitudinal study jointly measured baseline neurodevelopmental traits, environment, sleep, mood, substances, medication, and functioning. The cited studies support components, not the proposed integrated interaction.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** The apparent interaction could instead reflect one dominant condition, independent additive effects, measurement error, or ordinary fluctuation.
- **Calibrated claim:** Baseline traits and time-varying state/context variables can jointly influence functioning; their relative contribution must be estimated prospectively rather than assumed.
- **Falsifier:** Repeated prospective data showing that state and context variables add no reproducible predictive value beyond a stable trait model, or that trait measures add no value beyond a state-only model.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Contemporaneous developmental records, repeated objective function across settings, dated sleep/mood/substance/medication measures, and prespecified within-person analyses.

#### G2 — Context-dependent compensation can alter observable impairment

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** A task-literature review found altered exploration, switching, and reward-related performance in ADHD (PMID 40287643); school-based randomized interventions produced small functional gains (PMID 40761448); and an adult CBT meta-analysis found moderate global-function gains, especially occupationally (PMID 41936228).
- **Strongest opposing evidence or qualification:** These sources test task processes or interventions, not whether intelligence, autonomy, or environmental fit masked a disorder in one person. Effects were heterogeneous, and durability or far transfer was often limited.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Apparent compensation may reflect skill acquisition, treatment, selection into a well-matched setting, increased effort, social support, or measurement that misses hidden cost.
- **Calibrated claim:** Supports and task conditions can change observed performance without proving that every achievement is compensation or every difficulty is environmental mismatch.
- **Falsifier:** Matched repeated assessments showing stable performance, effort, and recovery cost despite withdrawal or addition of the proposed support or context.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Objective outcomes, effort and recovery cost, support dependence, setting changes, task characteristics, and repeated performance before and after prespecified support changes.

#### G3 — One coherent adaptive-constraint model explains the observations better than alternatives

- **Verdict:** Indeterminate
- **Certainty:** Very low.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Component-level evidence is compatible with persistence, context sensitivity, and state modulation (PMIDs 42365682, 40761448, and 42320790), so the model is internally plausible as a hypothesis.
- **Strongest opposing evidence or qualification:** No selected study compared the full model against bipolar disorder, sleep disorder, substance effects, anxiety/trauma, burnout, learned behavior, medical causes, or a multiple-cause formulation. PMID 30843223 also illustrates heterogeneity in the developmental-onset boundary of adult ADHD presentations.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Several independent or interacting causes may produce the same observations without a single latent adaptive-constraint mechanism.
- **Calibrated claim:** The model is a testable organizing hypothesis, not an established best explanation.
- **Falsifier:** A preregistered prospective model comparison in which a simpler competing model consistently predicts symptoms, timing, and objective function better than the integrated model.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Dated episodes and exposures, developmental collateral, objective cross-setting function, repeated alternative-model predictions, and out-of-sample prospective testing.

#### G4 — Retrospective reconstruction and confirmation bias can create apparent coherence

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** In an 83-clinician diagnostic vignette study, clinicians generated more confirming than disconfirming questions, and supervisors considered more differential diagnoses (PMID 35609443; d=1.57). Retrospective developmental-onset classifications can also differ from prospective cohort evidence (PMID 30843223).
- **Strongest opposing evidence or qualification:** The vignette study did not measure autobiographical recall, and neither source shows that bias created this individual's narrative. A coherent retrospective account can also contain accurate observations.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** Apparent coherence may reflect a genuinely recurring pattern, incomplete contemporaneous records, or useful retrospective insight rather than bias.
- **Calibrated claim:** Retrospective narratives and diagnostic interviews are vulnerable to confirmation and recall biases; collateral records and prospective measurement reduce, but do not eliminate, them.
- **Falsifier:** Contemporaneous records and independent collateral that closely reproduce the retrospective timeline, plus structured disconfirming inquiry that leaves the core model's predictions intact.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Original school and medical records, safe collateral, a blinded structured history, explicit disconfirming questions, and prospective tracking.

#### G5 — Multiple independent or interacting causes can outperform one unifying model

- **Verdict:** Partially supported
- **Certainty:** Low.
- **Directness to this case:** Very low.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate.
- **Strongest support:** Current NICE bipolar guidance requires assessment of ADHD, substance misuse, medication, and physical differential diagnoses, and prospective ADHD research shows heterogeneous developmental presentations (PMID 30843223). Together they support keeping plural explanations active.
- **Strongest opposing evidence or qualification:** Neither source compared a plural model with the proposed unified model in this individual, and multiple labels can overfit sparse retrospective data.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** One well-specified disorder or exposure could account parsimoniously for most observations once timing and measurement are improved.
- **Calibrated claim:** Multiple causes should remain active alternatives until a dated longitudinal record discriminates among them.
- **Falsifier:** Prospective evidence that one prespecified mechanism accurately predicts onset, remission, cross-setting function, and response across time while additional causes add no explanatory value.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Specialist differential assessment, dated exposure and episode history, collateral records, objective function, and prospective comparison of single- versus multiple-cause predictions.

#### G6 — Group-level literature cannot establish individual diagnosis or causation

- **Verdict:** Supported
- **Certainty:** High.
- **Directness to this case:** High.
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** High.
- **Strongest support:** NICE ADHD guidance requires specialist assessment, developmental manifestations, impairment across settings, and consideration of alternatives; NICE bipolar guidance similarly requires individual clinical and differential assessment. The included studies estimate group associations or intervention averages, not this person's diagnosis or causal history.
- **Strongest opposing evidence or qualification:** Group evidence can legitimately inform prior probabilities, assessment priorities, and safety considerations; the boundary concerns individual proof, not clinical irrelevance.
- **Alternative explanation (unverified hypothesis unless cited):** A future highly specific and validated individual-level biomarker could narrow this limitation, but no such result was established in this review.
- **Calibrated claim:** Literature can establish plausibility, base rates, and safety-relevant alternatives; diagnosis and causal attribution require qualified clinical evaluation, collateral history, and prospective observation.
- **Falsifier:** A validated individual-level test with near-perfect discrimination and demonstrated causal identification for the target condition would weaken this boundary.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Qualified diagnostic assessment, developmental and episode chronology, collateral records, objective measures, and prospective observation.

### Global null hypothesis

The null hypothesis is **not rejected**. The evidence supports several components of the adaptive-constraint model, but it does not show that one coherent neurodevelopmental explanation outperforms mood, sleep, substance/medication, anxiety/trauma, burnout, learned behavior, recall bias, or a plural formulation for this individual.

## Integrated adversarial synthesis

### Components with the strongest support

- Developmental persistence and multi-setting burden are established possibilities in ADHD, although remission and heterogeneous adult outcomes are common.
- ADHD and autism are each associated with social-function difficulty; internalizing symptoms, bullying, stigma, conflict, and co-occurring conditions can add burden.
- Sleep/circadian disturbance is clinically relevant and often bidirectional with mood and attention.
- Cannabis—especially frequent/high-THC exposure—is associated with psychosis and adverse mood/cognitive outcomes.
- Prescribed stimulants have uncommon but serious psychiatric risks that vary by agent, dose, vulnerability, and mood-stabilization context.

### Plausible but weakly evidenced

- High intelligence or achievement as a mechanism of delayed detection or masking.
- General person-environment fit as a causal moderator beyond specific task/reward effects.
- Durable real-world benefit from generic checklists, reminders, occupational redesign, or AI-assisted organization.
- Stable neurodevelopmental “strengths” in creativity, hyperfocus, or pattern recognition.
- Emotional compensation as a general function of maladaptive daydreaming.

### Overstated or unsupported formulations

- Achievement alone proves masking or compensation.
- A coherent life narrative establishes one neurodevelopmental cause.
- ADHD alone explains social-communication or coordination difficulty.
- Sleep loss, cannabis, stimulants, or nicotine necessarily explain every state change—or, conversely, are irrelevant because baseline traits exist.
- Group associations determine the individual's diagnosis or causal history.

### Nonspecific findings

Executive difficulty, rapid speech, distractibility, emotional dysregulation, confidence, unusual salience, sleep change, social conflict, and variable performance can occur across ADHD, autism, bipolar states, anxiety/trauma, depression, sleep disorders, substance/medication effects, burnout, and ordinary contextual variation. Temporal course and collateral evidence are more discriminating than symptom resemblance.

### What would materially weaken the central model

- Reliable childhood and adolescent records showing intact attention, organization, social function, and coordination across settings.
- Clear adult onset after a mood, sleep, substance, medication, medical, or occupational change with durable resolution when that factor normalizes.
- Stable impairment across very different environments with no task/context moderation.
- Achievement obtained efficiently and independently without unusual effort, support, variability, or recovery cost.
- Prospective data showing proposed scaffolds do not reproducibly improve objective outcomes.

### Data most likely to discriminate explanations

- Contemporaneous childhood records and safe collateral history.
- Structured specialist ADHD and bipolar assessment; autism/DCD assessment when indicated.
- A dated medication, substance, sleep, and mood timeline.
- Prospective daily measures of sleep, energy, speech, unusual certainty/salience, attention, task demands, and objective function.
- Setting-specific impairment and support/effort measures.
- Medical/neurological and sleep-disorder evaluation when clinically indicated.

## Psychiatrist-facing safety boundary

Current NICE bipolar guidance directs urgent specialist assessment when mania or severe depression is suspected and requires consideration of ADHD, substance misuse, medications, and physical differential diagnoses. This report does not recommend starting, stopping, or changing any medication or substance. Intentional sleep deprivation, cannabis/stimulant/nicotine rechallenge, or unsupervised medication experimentation would be unsafe ways to test the model.
