## Executive synthesis

Strongest: developmental persistence of ADHD-related impairment (18-year prospective cohort with sibling and control groups; four-wave European transition cohort), and the fact that structured external support changes observable functioning (adult CBT meta-analysis, n=5152, global functioning SMD 0.47 rising to 0.67; organizational-skills training g=0.54-0.83). Also supported: widespread co-occurrence with non-specific markers, and the boundary that group data cannot establish individual causation.

Weakest: intelligence-as-masking (indeterminate; no record tested intelligence as a predictor of delayed recognition, and higher childhood IQ predicted lower persistence, OR 0.99); hyperfocus (no eligible measurement evidence); divergent thinking (only a review with no neurodevelopmental sample); AI-assisted scaffolding (no durable-benefit evidence); interest- and urgency-dependent attention (never experimentally manipulated); adult DCD persistence (one small cross-sectional self-report study); daydreaming as emotional compensation (function never measured; the sole cross-lagged study ran the opposite way).

Unresolved: direction of effect. ADHD symptoms predicted later sleep disturbance as often as the reverse; covarying current mania severity abolished apparently trait-like attention deficits; polygenic liability predicted reported bullying exposure; medication-emergent activation is not reliably separable from mood episodes; longer rather than shorter sleep predicted bipolar or psychotic transition in one youth cohort.

No eligible record performed the model comparison the unifying claim asserts, and observed non-specificity favours plurality. The global null is not rejected: several components are provisionally supported individually, but the integrated adaptive-constraint model is not established as superior to plural or alternative explanations, and nothing here bears on any individual diagnosis.

**Global null hypothesis:** Not Rejected.

## Module A: Developmental persistence, executive function, and compensation

Across 14 abstract-level records, the persistence limb (H1.1-H1.4) is best supported: an 18-year prospective follow-up, an 8-year clinical follow-up, a four-wave European transition cohort and a large longitudinal community cohort converge on childhood-onset, adolescent- and adult-persisting, multi-domain burden. Certainty is moderate, not high: effects are often small or unreported, informant overlap is common, samples are service-attending, and an older-adult review found mixed objective executive findings. Counterclaim H1.N1 is partially supported: stimulant-associated psychosis/bipolar occurrence and marked two-year diagnostic instability show state, medication and comorbidity factors can generate or amplify adult difficulty, though none demonstrates de novo adult onset of a lifelong-appearing profile. The intelligence-compensation limb (H2) is weaker. Only small uncontrolled data support impairment coexisting with high ability, while an overlap-controlled meta-analysis of 115 studies links inattention to weaker rather than preserved cognition, and higher childhood IQ predicted lower persistence. Delayed recognition is documented, but demonstrated drivers are camouflaging, gendered presentation and instrument insensitivity, not intelligence. H2.2 and H2.3 are therefore indeterminate, H2.1 and H2.N1 partially supported, and qualification H2.4 supported. No diagnostic, causal or treatment inference about any individual is made.


### H1.1 — ADHD-related executive difficulties can begin in childhood.

- **Verdict:** Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** Direct
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** High
- **Strongest support:** The 18-year follow-up cohort (PMID 42365682) recruited on the basis of childhood ADHD and documented downstream neurocognitive and functional differences, and the ABCD longitudinal clustering study (PMID 42336134) showed that objectively measured executive profiles in late childhood track attention problems and ADHD diagnosis. (PMID 42365682, PMID 42336134)
- **Strongest opposition:** No eligible record contradicts childhood onset, but the ABCD effects were very small (rank-biserial r=0.06-0.11) and executive profile membership was only moderately stable (κ=0.41), so childhood executive difficulty is neither uniform nor a stable trait marker. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Executive-task differences are detectable in childhood ADHD at the group level, but they are small, overlapping with typically developing peers, and better described as a probabilistic correlate than a defining feature. (PMID 42336134)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Measurement artefact from parent-reported behavioural outcomes correlated with symptom ratings; Shared method variance between attention ratings and diagnosis; Non-ADHD contributors to childhood executive weakness such as sleep, anxiety or socioeconomic adversity
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Small effect sizes in the largest longitudinal cohort; Only moderate temporal stability of executive clusters; Single-informant behavioural outcomes; No eligible record links childhood executive testing to this individual's era or setting
- **Calibrated claim:** Group-level evidence indicates that executive-task and attention differences associated with ADHD are already measurable in childhood, though effects are small and heterogeneous rather than universal.
- **Falsifier:** Large longitudinal cohorts showing that childhood executive-task performance in ADHD is indistinguishable from controls once informant overlap, comorbidity and socioeconomic factors are controlled would materially weaken the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** School records, report cards, prior psychoeducational testing, and a structured developmental interview with family collateral establishing pre-age-12 symptoms and impairment.


### H1.2 — ADHD-related executive difficulties can persist through adolescence.

- **Verdict:** Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** Direct
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** High
- **Strongest support:** The MILESTONE cohort (PMID 42481172) documented persisting symptom severity and functional impairment across four waves spanning the adolescent-to-adult service transition, and meta-analytic work on childhood predictors (PMIDs 40287009, 40801243) is premised on measurable, quantifiable persistence into later developmental stages. (PMID 42481172, PMID 42365682, PMID 41985649, PMID 42336134)
- **Strongest opposition:** A 24-month diagnostic-stability cohort (PMID 41755570) found low stability of the ADHD diagnostic label in adolescents after a first psychiatric emergency contact, and the comorbidity meta-analysis (PMID 40801243) showed persistence associations attenuating or disappearing after adjustment and appearing only with parent-reported data. (PMID 41755570)
- **Qualified interpretation:** ADHD-related difficulties commonly persist through adolescence, but persistence is partial, informant-dependent, and confounded by comorbidity; a substantial minority remit or are rediagnosed. (PMID 40801243, PMID 40287009)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Informant continuity rather than symptom continuity (same parent rating at both time points); Comorbid externalizing or internalizing conditions driving apparent persistence; Diagnostic label inertia in clinical records; Service-attending samples over-representing chronic cases
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Parent-report dependence for persistence associations; Attrition across multi-year follow-up not quantified in abstracts; Emergency-service and clinic samples with selection bias; ADHD not separated from other diagnoses in the transition cohort
- **Calibrated claim:** ADHD-related symptoms and impairment persist through adolescence in a substantial proportion of affected individuals, with persistence estimates sensitive to informant, comorbidity adjustment and sampling frame.
- **Falsifier:** Population-based multi-informant longitudinal data showing that adolescent persistence of ADHD symptoms disappears once comorbidity, informant and referral bias are accounted for.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Adolescent school and disciplinary records, teacher reports, any prior evaluations, and a retrospective structured interview with a collateral informant covering ages 12-18.


### H1.3 — ADHD-related executive difficulties can persist into adulthood.

- **Verdict:** Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** Direct
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** High
- **Strongest support:** The 18-year prospective follow-up (PMID 42365682) found adults with childhood ADHD worse than controls on more than 60% of outcomes across psychiatric, cognitive, occupational, adaptive and health domains, while unaffected siblings resembled controls; a preregistered neuroimaging review in adults aged 35+ (PMID 42472451) found ADHD-associated brain alterations persisting into later life. (PMID 42365682, PMID 41985649, PMID 42472451, PMID 41680598, PMID 42481172)
- **Strongest opposition:** A systematic review of older adults with ADHD (PMID 41146423) found mixed executive findings, with some studies showing working memory performance similar to or better than younger adults with ADHD, indicating that objective executive impairment does not persist uniformly. (PMID 41146423)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Symptoms and broad functional impairment persist into adulthood for many, but the specific claim that objectively measured executive dysfunction persists is less consistently supported than the claim that functional and symptomatic burden persists. (PMID 41146423, PMID 40287009, PMID 41989410)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Accumulated psychiatric comorbidity rather than persisting core executive deficits; Self-report and clinician expectancy effects in adult ADHD assessment; Attrition favouring retention of more impaired participants; Age-related cognitive change confounding older-adult findings
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Objective versus self-reported executive outcomes not consistently separated; Few and heterogeneous studies in the older-adult review; Neuroimaging findings are indirect proxies for functional impairment; Effect sizes frequently unreported in abstracts
- **Calibrated claim:** ADHD-related symptoms and cross-domain functional impairment frequently persist into adulthood; evidence that laboratory-measured executive dysfunction persists with equal consistency is weaker and mixed.
- **Falsifier:** Prospective cohorts with objective neuropsychological testing showing that adults with childhood ADHD perform within the normal range on executive measures once comorbidity and medication are controlled.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Standardised adult ADHD diagnostic interview with impairment ratings across settings, objective neuropsychological testing during euthymia and abstinence, and collateral observation from a partner or colleague.


### H1.4 — Persistent ADHD traits can impair more than one life setting.

- **Verdict:** Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** Direct
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** High
- **Strongest support:** The 18-year follow-up (PMID 42365682) reported worse adult outcomes spanning psychiatric status, behavioural/emotional problems, academic and professional functioning, adaptive functioning, neurocognition, physical health and healthcare use, indicating impairment across multiple settings simultaneously. (PMID 42365682, PMID 41985649, PMID 42481172, PMID 41680598)
- **Strongest opposition:** No eligible record directly contradicts multi-setting impairment, but the adult-diagnosis chart review (PMID 41680598) shows presentations dominated by comorbid complaints, and the transition cohort (PMID 42481172) does not isolate ADHD, so cross-setting impairment may reflect comorbidity burden as much as ADHD itself. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Persistent ADHD is associated with impairment in several domains concurrently, but the literature rarely disentangles ADHD-attributable impairment from that contributed by depression, anxiety, substance use and personality pathology. (PMID 41680598, PMID 42481172)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Comorbid psychiatric conditions producing the multi-domain pattern; Reverse causation from occupational or relational failure to symptom reporting; Referral bias toward multiply impaired clinic attenders; Common-method bias when the same informant rates all domains
- **Methodological weaknesses:** ADHD-specific effect estimates absent from several abstracts; Non-separable ADHD subgroups in mixed diagnostic cohorts; Uncontrolled retrospective chart data; Domain outcomes often self-reported
- **Calibrated claim:** Persistent ADHD is associated at group level with concurrent impairment across occupational, educational, social, adaptive and health domains, although comorbidity contributes substantially to this multi-domain burden.
- **Falsifier:** Cohort data showing that adults with persistent ADHD are impaired in only one domain, or that multi-domain impairment is fully explained by comorbid conditions after adjustment.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Setting-specific impairment ratings (work, home, relationships, self-care), occupational performance records, and collateral reports from more than one setting.


### H1.N1 — Adult difficulties may instead begin after mood, sleep, trauma, substance, medication, or occupational changes.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** High
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** A large preregistered meta-analysis (PMID 40900605) documented non-negligible occurrence of psychotic symptoms, psychotic disorder and bipolar disorder among stimulant-treated individuals with ADHD, and a 24-month cohort (PMID 41755570) showed marked instability of psychiatric diagnoses including ADHD, both consistent with state-, medication- or comorbidity-driven adult presentations. (PMID 40900605, PMID 41755570, PMID 40801243)
- **Strongest opposition:** The 18-year follow-up (PMID 42365682) found that siblings without childhood ADHD functioned comparably to controls and that adults with childhood ADHD were impaired across domains, which is more consistent with a developmental trajectory than with adult-onset causes. (PMID 42365682)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Mood, substance, medication and comorbidity factors are demonstrably capable of producing or amplifying adult cognitive and behavioural difficulty, but no eligible record demonstrates that such factors typically generate a full lifelong-appearing ADHD picture de novo in adulthood. (PMID 40801243, PMID 41755570)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Genuine childhood-onset ADHD with delayed recognition; Confounding by indication in stimulant-exposure studies; Retrospective recall bias inflating apparent childhood onset; Comorbidity emerging as a consequence rather than a cause of persistent ADHD
- **Methodological weaknesses:** I²>95% heterogeneity and absent unexposed comparators in the stimulant meta-analysis; Diagnostic-label stability is not equivalent to symptom onset timing; Emergency-service sampling; Causality explicitly disclaimed by primary authors
- **Calibrated claim:** Mood episodes, sleep disruption, substances, medication effects and comorbid conditions can plausibly produce or amplify adult executive and behavioural difficulty, and cannot be excluded as partial or alternative explanations without documented childhood-onset evidence.
- **Falsifier:** Documented, collateral-corroborated childhood-onset impairment predating any mood episode, substance use, medication exposure or occupational stressor would substantially weaken this counterclaim for this individual.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** A dated timeline of mood episodes, sleep, THC, nicotine, prescribed stimulants and psychotropics aligned against symptom and functioning changes; prospective mood and sleep charting; and childhood records establishing onset before any of these exposures.


### H2.1 — High academic or intellectual achievement is compatible with clinically meaningful impairment.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** A retrospective adult ADHD treatment cohort stratified by full-scale IQ (PMID 41089022) reported clinically significant symptoms and functional impairment, with improvement, across all IQ strata including above-average IQ, indicating that impairment and higher measured ability can co-occur. (PMID 41089022)
- **Strongest opposition:** A 115-study meta-analysis with symptom-overlap control (PMID 41989410) found inattention symptoms associated with weaker measured intelligence, perceptual reasoning, working memory and inhibition, so at group level higher ability tends to accompany fewer inattentive symptoms rather than coexisting with equivalent impairment. (PMID 41989410)
- **Qualified interpretation:** High ability does not exclude clinically meaningful impairment in individual cases, but group-level data show an inverse association between inattention and measured intelligence; co-occurrence must be demonstrated case by case rather than assumed. (PMID 41989410)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Referral bias: high-ability adults who reach clinic are those with genuine impairment; Restriction of range in small IQ-stratified samples; Self-report inflation of impairment in articulate, psychologically minded individuals; Impairment attributable to comorbid conditions rather than ADHD
- **Methodological weaknesses:** n=24 uncontrolled retrospective sample with post hoc IQ subgrouping; No effect estimates reported for the IQ-stratified analysis; Meta-analytic evidence exclusively cross-sectional; No study measured achievement and impairment jointly within high-achieving adults
- **Calibrated claim:** Clinically meaningful ADHD-related impairment can coexist with average or above-average measured intelligence in individual cases, though group-level data associate higher inattention with lower rather than preserved cognitive performance.
- **Falsifier:** Adequately powered studies of high-IQ or high-achieving adults showing that ADHD-attributable functional impairment is absent once comorbidity and self-report bias are controlled.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Formal cognitive testing alongside standardised cross-setting impairment ratings and objective performance records (error rates, missed deadlines, incident reports) to show impairment relative to ability rather than to population norms.


### H2.2 — Higher intelligence can delay recognition of ADHD or autistic impairment.

- **Verdict:** Indeterminate
- **Certainty:** Very Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** A systematic review of autism in females (PMID 42052784) found consistently delayed diagnosis attributable to camouflaging and reduced instrument sensitivity to less externalizing presentations, and an adult-diagnosis chart review (PMID 41680598) found that only 56.3% of adults newly diagnosed with ADHD presented because of ADHD symptoms, indicating that recognition is frequently delayed. (PMID 42052784, PMID 41680598)
- **Strongest opposition:** The predictor meta-analysis (PMID 40287009) found that higher childhood IQ predicted lower, not higher, adult ADHD persistence, which is at least as consistent with genuine remission or lower baseline severity as with concealed persisting impairment. (PMID 40287009)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Delayed recognition of neurodevelopmental conditions is well documented, but the demonstrated drivers in these records are gendered presentation, camouflaging and instrument insensitivity, not intelligence specifically; no eligible record tests intelligence as a cause of delayed ADHD recognition. (PMID 40287009)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Service access, help-seeking behaviour and stigma delaying diagnosis; Historical diagnostic practice not recognising adult ADHD; Genuinely milder childhood presentation not meeting thresholds; Lower symptom severity co-occurring with higher ability
- **Methodological weaknesses:** No eligible study measured intelligence as a predictor of age at ADHD diagnosis; Autism-derived camouflaging evidence extrapolated to ADHD; Camouflaging largely inferred from self-report; Retrospective, uncontrolled diagnostic-timing data
- **Calibrated claim:** Delayed recognition of neurodevelopmental conditions is well documented and plausibly related to compensatory presentation, but there is no eligible direct evidence in this module that higher intelligence specifically delays recognition of ADHD or autism.
- **Falsifier:** Cohort studies finding no association between measured intelligence or achievement and age at first ADHD or autism diagnosis after adjusting for symptom severity, sex and service access would render the claim unsupported.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Documented age at first symptom recognition versus first evaluation, prior teacher or clinician concerns that were not acted on, childhood IQ or achievement testing, and records of any earlier referrals declined or closed.


### H2.3 — Compensation can preserve achievement while increasing effort or instability.

- **Verdict:** Indeterminate
- **Certainty:** Very Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** The autism gender-bias review (PMID 42052784) describes social camouflaging as a documented process by which outward presentation is maintained while identification is delayed, and the adult neuroimaging review (PMID 42472451) reported frontal hypoactivation with parietal hyperactivation interpreted as compensatory neural recruitment. (PMID 42052784, PMID 42472451)
- **Strongest opposition:** No eligible record measured effort, fatigue or performance instability concurrently with preserved achievement; the IQ-stratified CBT cohort (PMID 41089022) reported no effort or instability outcome, so the cost side of the compensation hypothesis is untested here. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Compensation is a coherent and partially evidenced mechanism at the neural and behavioural-presentation level, but the specific proposition that it preserves achievement at the price of measurable extra effort or instability is not tested by any eligible abstract. (PMID 41089022, PMID 42472451)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Compensatory activation reflecting task difficulty or strategy differences rather than effortful masking; Preserved achievement reflecting genuinely intact functioning; Retrospective attribution of remembered effort to a compensation narrative; Effort perceptions driven by anxiety or perfectionism
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Neural compensation inferred from activation patterns without behavioural effort measures; Cross-sectional neuroimaging designs; Camouflaging assessed largely by self-report; No within-person effort-versus-output data in any included record
- **Calibrated claim:** Compensatory processes are plausibly documented at neural and presentation levels, but eligible evidence does not establish that compensation preserves achievement specifically at the cost of increased effort or performance instability.
- **Falsifier:** Within-person studies measuring effort, physiological load and output showing that high-achieving adults with ADHD traits expend no more effort than matched controls for equivalent output.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Prospective task-level logs recording task type, time spent, subjective effort, errors and completion, compared against matched output; sleep and recovery data; and repeated measures across high- and low-demand periods.


### H2.4 — Achievement alone does not establish compensation or masking.

- **Verdict:** Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** High
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** High
- **Strongest support:** The predictor meta-analysis (PMID 40287009) showed higher childhood IQ predicting lower adult ADHD persistence, and the overlap-controlled meta-analysis (PMID 41989410) showed inattention associated with weaker rather than preserved cognitive performance; both indicate that achievement or ability data alone are compatible with genuinely lower symptom burden rather than concealed impairment. (PMID 40287009, PMID 41989410)
- **Strongest opposition:** The autism review (PMID 42052784) confirms that camouflaging genuinely occurs and delays diagnosis, so achievement in the presence of documented compensatory behaviours is not evidence against impairment either. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Achievement is an ambiguous datum: it is consistent both with successful compensation and with genuinely preserved functioning, and can only be interpreted when paired with independent measures of effort, impairment and developmental history. (PMID 42052784)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** High achievement reflecting genuinely efficient functioning; High achievement reflecting favourable environments and external structure; High achievement reflecting selective reporting of successes; High achievement coexisting with unrecognised impairment (compensation)
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Inference drawn across constructs (autism camouflaging, ADHD persistence, dimensional cognition); No eligible record operationalises masking with an achievement-independent criterion; Cross-sectional design of the dimensional meta-analysis; Confounding by symptom severity in the IQ-persistence association
- **Calibrated claim:** Achievement or academic success alone is not diagnostic evidence for or against compensation or masking; establishing compensation requires independent measures of effort, cross-setting impairment and developmental history.
- **Falsifier:** Validated evidence that a specific achievement pattern reliably discriminates compensated impairment from unimpaired functioning would make achievement alone informative and weaken this qualification.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Objective effort and error records paired with achievement, standardised impairment ratings, collateral history, and evidence of hidden supports (extensions, accommodations, informal help) alongside documented outcomes.


### H2.N1 — Observed achievement may reflect efficient independent functioning rather than hidden impairment.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** Higher childhood IQ was associated with lower odds of adult ADHD persistence (PMID 40287009, OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.98-1.00), and inattention was associated with weaker rather than preserved measured cognitive performance across 115 studies (PMID 41989410), both consistent with achievement indexing genuinely better functioning. (PMID 40287009, PMID 41989410)
- **Strongest opposition:** The IQ-stratified adult treatment cohort (PMID 41089022) found clinically significant impairment across all IQ strata including above-average IQ, showing that achievement does not guarantee absence of impairment. (PMID 41089022)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Achievement can legitimately reflect efficient independent functioning, and group-level data lean that way; however, this counterclaim is not established for any individual, since impairment is documented in high-ability adults presenting clinically. (PMID 41989410)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Clinical referral bias creating apparent impairment among high-ability adults; Effect of childhood IQ mediated by symptom severity rather than by efficiency; Achievement sustained by external scaffolding rather than by intrinsic efficiency; Historical achievement not generalising to current occupational demands
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Near-null per-unit IQ effect with a confidence interval touching 1.00; Exclusively cross-sectional dimensional meta-analysis; Very small, uncontrolled high-IQ subgroup evidence on the opposing side; No study directly contrasts efficient functioning against compensated functioning
- **Calibrated claim:** Group-level data are consistent with achievement partly reflecting genuinely efficient functioning and lower symptom burden, but they do not exclude coexisting clinically meaningful impairment in an individual high-achieving adult.
- **Falsifier:** Demonstration of objectively verified, cross-setting, ability-adjusted functional impairment with collateral corroboration would weaken this counterclaim for this individual.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Ability-referenced impairment assessment (performance relative to measured capacity), documentation of accommodations or hidden support used to achieve outcomes, and collateral appraisal of efficiency versus effort.


## Module B: ADHD-autism-DCD overlap and social functioning

Across 14 abstract-level records, the best-supported elements are that ADHD symptoms are associated with, and longitudinally precede, peer victimisation and social-skill difficulty (41890162, 41507887, 41989410), and that neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions commonly co-occur with largely non-specific markers, favouring a plural formulation (41638598, 41121040, 42508582). Autistic social-communication differences versus neurotypical peers are large and replicable (42120904, 41695975), but no eligible record tested whether autistic features add variance beyond ADHD, and masking-related distress also occurs in non-autistic samples (42311563). Competing explanations are credible but unproven: adult autism and personality-disorder features overlap enough to compromise diagnostic accuracy (42319023), and autistic traits share genetic liability with anxiety and depression (42508582); no record addressed trauma or substances. Motor claims are weakest: no direct ADHD-DCD comorbidity estimate, only one small cross-sectional adult self-report study (42293966), and overlap-controlled data showing inattention itself relates to perceptual-motor weakness while hyperactivity-impulsivity relates to better motor coordination (41989410, 41121040). Perinatal risk, emotion regulation and activity/practice are demonstrated competing explanations for motor findings (41638598, 41654285). No individual diagnosis, causal attribution or treatment guidance follows.


### H5.1 — ADHD is associated with social and peer difficulties.

- **Verdict:** Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** A three-wave cohort of 20,137 adolescents found baseline ADHD symptoms predicted later peer bullying victimisation and depressive symptoms, with these mediators accounting for about one third of the total effect on the distal outcome (41890162). A meta-analysis of 115 studies found ADHD hyperactivity-impulsivity symptoms associated with weaker social cognition even after controlling overlap with inattention and cognitive disengagement (41989410), and ADHD symptom severity co-varied with parent-rated social skills and communication interest in 141 preschoolers (41507887). (PMID 41890162, PMID 41507887)
- **Strongest opposition:** No extracted record isolates ADHD from comorbid conditions or studies diagnosed adults. The overlap-controlled meta-analysis shows the social-cognitive association is dimension-specific rather than uniform across ADHD presentations (41989410), and the longitudinal pathway ran substantially through depressive symptoms and peer victimisation, so part of the association reflects mood and social-context processes rather than ADHD per se (41890162). (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** ADHD symptoms are reliably associated with, and in adolescent longitudinal data temporally precede, peer victimisation and social-skill difficulties; the association is dimension-specific, partly mediated by depressive symptoms, moderated by sex and developmental stage, and demonstrated with rating scales rather than diagnostic interviews. (PMID 41989410)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Co-occurring depressive or anxiety symptoms drive both symptom reports and social difficulty; Oppositional/conduct features rather than core ADHD account for peer rejection; Shared informant or self-report method variance inflates symptom-outcome correlations; Peer-group and school context effects (including stigma) rather than individual traits
- **Methodological weaknesses:** ADHD defined by screening scales, not clinician diagnosis; Common-method bias from single-informant measurement; Cross-sectional designs in the meta-analysed and preschool evidence; Paediatric and adolescent samples with no adult data in the extracted set
- **Calibrated claim:** ADHD symptoms are consistently associated with peer and social difficulties and, in large adolescent longitudinal data, precede peer victimisation; the association is presentation-specific and partly mediated by depressive symptoms, and the extracted evidence does not include diagnosed adult samples.
- **Falsifier:** Adequately powered longitudinal studies of clinician-diagnosed adults showing no association between ADHD and peer/social difficulty once comorbid mood, anxiety and social-context factors are adjusted, or showing the association reverses when informant-independent social outcomes are used.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Structured adult ADHD diagnostic interview with an informant, collateral childhood peer and school history, standardised impairment/social-functioning ratings across several settings, and repeat measurement during a euthymic, abstinent period to separate mood-driven from trait-level social difficulty.


### H5.2 — Autistic features can explain additional social-communication variance beyond ADHD.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** A synthesis of 2,622 behavioural studies comparing 94,114 clinically diagnosed autistic with 172,847 neurotypical individuals found substantial social-functioning differences (Hedges' g = -0.744, 95% CI -0.797 to -0.690) across 22 components, with earliest divergence in motivation-based processes (42120904). A coordinate-based meta-analysis found consistent left superior/middle temporal hypoactivation during figurative language comprehension in ASD, relevant to reported literal interpretation (41695975). (PMID 42120904, PMID 41695975)
- **Strongest opposition:** No extracted record tested whether autistic features add social-communication variance over and above ADHD. A review of executive function and emotion regulation across autism, ADHD and their co-occurrence found comparable associations in all groups and only two studies of the co-occurring phenotype (41579913), and camouflaging-mental health associations were present in non-autistic samples too, undermining the specificity of masking-based reasoning (42311563). (PMID 42311563)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Autistic social-communication and non-literal-language differences relative to neurotypical peers are large and replicable, but the incremental-variance question in the claim is untested here because eligible records lacked ADHD comparators; expert review also advises confirming that ADHD features are not better explained by ASD before diagnosing both (41388592). (PMID 41579913, PMID 41388592)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** ADHD-related executive and social-cognitive deficits account for the same variance; Anxiety, language ability or IQ confound apparent social-communication differences; Item overlap between ADHD and autism instruments creates artefactual incremental variance; Retrospective self-identification with autistic descriptions in an informed adult
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Absent ADHD or DCD comparator groups in the supporting syntheses; Neuroimaging surrogate outcomes with no behavioural comprehension estimates; Marked construct and measurement heterogeneity across pooled studies; Very few studies of the co-occurring autism/ADHD phenotype
- **Calibrated claim:** Autistic individuals differ substantially from neurotypical peers across many social-communication domains, but the extracted evidence does not quantify how much social-communication variance autistic features explain beyond ADHD, and shared executive/emotional pathways plus non-specific masking effects mean this incremental claim remains untested.
- **Falsifier:** Studies directly comparing ADHD-only, autism-only and co-occurring adults that show autistic features contribute no unique variance to social-communication outcomes after adjusting for ADHD severity, IQ, language and anxiety.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** An autism-specific developmental and clinical assessment including informant developmental history and childhood records, pragmatic-language and non-literal comprehension measures, and documentation of restricted/repetitive features independent of current mood state.


### H5.3 — Anxiety, trauma, mood state, substances, or conflict can explain social difficulties.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** An adult-focused systematic review (550 clinical participants) found that feature overlap between autism and personality disorders, especially borderline personality disorder, compromises diagnostic accuracy, with emotional instability, impulsivity and fear of abandonment producing social difficulty through non-autistic mechanisms (42319023). Longitudinal adolescent data showed depressive symptoms mediating ADHD-related social outcomes (41890162), and camouflaging-related distress occurred in non-autistic as well as autistic samples (42311563). (PMID 42319023, PMID 41890162)
- **Strongest opposition:** No extracted record shows that anxiety, mood, trauma or substance factors fully account for social difficulties. Autistic-neurotypical social differences appear from around six months of age, before typical onset of most psychiatric conditions (42120904), and twin data indicate shared genetic and non-shared environmental liability (rA = 0.42) rather than internalising symptoms explaining autistic traits (42508582). No extracted record addressed substance use or trauma at all. (PMID 42120904)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Anxiety, depressive, personality-disorder and emotion-regulation features co-occur with, share aetiology with, and can confound the assessment of social difficulty; co-occurrence and shared liability are not the same as explanation, and executive-emotional pathways are common to several conditions (41579913). (PMID 42311563, PMID 42508582, PMID 41579913)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Bidirectional causation between social difficulty and internalising symptoms; Shared genetic liability generating both trait and symptom domains; Diagnostic overshadowing in either direction during clinical assessment; State-dependent amplification during mood episodes rather than a stable explanation
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Small aggregate samples and no meta-analysis in the adult differential-diagnosis review; Trait-level rather than diagnostic measurement in the twin evidence; Predominantly cross-sectional and self-report data across records; No extracted evidence on trauma, cannabis, stimulant or nicotine effects on social functioning
- **Calibrated claim:** Anxiety, depressive and personality-disorder features frequently co-occur with, share aetiology with, and can be mistaken for neurodevelopmental social difficulty in adults, but the extracted evidence does not show they fully explain such difficulty, and it contains no data on trauma or substance-related contributions.
- **Falsifier:** Prospective within-person data showing that social-communication difficulties remit substantially during sustained euthymia, abstinence and low anxiety, with collateral confirmation of normal social functioning in those periods.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Daily mood/anxiety charting with sleep, a dated substance and medication timeline, structured trauma history, personality-disorder-sensitive assessment, and repeated standardised social-functioning measures during a documented euthymic abstinent interval.


### H5.4 — Multiple interacting conditions may explain social difficulties better than one diagnosis.

- **Verdict:** Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** In a national cohort assessed at age 12, 59% of extremely preterm children with moderate/severe neurodevelopmental disability had at least two co-occurring conditions, with ASD, ADHD and DCD all substantially elevated versus term controls (41638598). Oculomotor markers of inattention were shared across ADHD, autism, DCD, OCD, borderline personality disorder and psychosis rather than being disorder-specific (41121040), and twin data indicate shared liability across autistic traits and internalising symptoms (42508582). (PMID 41638598, PMID 41121040, PMID 42508582, PMID 42319023)
- **Strongest opposition:** Overlap does not mean indistinguishability: with symptom overlap statistically controlled, ADHD-inattention, ADHD-hyperactivity/impulsivity and cognitive disengagement showed partly dissociable cognitive profiles, including opposite directions of association with motor coordination (41989410). Expert review also cautions that comorbid diagnoses should be made only after differential assessment establishing that one condition does not better explain the presentation (41388592). (PMID 41989410)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Group-level co-occurrence and shared markers are common enough that a plural, time-sensitive formulation is defensible, but cohort multimorbidity rates cannot establish that any particular individual requires multiple diagnoses, and dimension-level dissociations show the constructs remain separable. (PMID 41388592, PMID 41579913)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** One primary condition with secondary features that mimic additional diagnoses; Ascertainment and referral bias inflating apparent comorbidity in clinical samples; Instrument item overlap generating artefactual co-occurrence; Shared perinatal or genetic liability producing correlated but non-causal phenotypes
- **Methodological weaknesses:** High-risk perinatal cohort limits generalisability to idiopathic presentations; Narrative reviews without pooling or matched diagnostic comparisons; Trait-level and register-based diagnostic ascertainment; Absence of adult multimorbidity data in the extracted set
- **Calibrated claim:** Co-occurrence across neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions is common at group level and shared markers are frequently non-specific, which supports a plural formulation as a working hypothesis; this does not establish which combination applies to an individual, and constructs remain partly dissociable when overlap is controlled.
- **Falsifier:** A careful sequential differential assessment in which a single diagnosis parsimoniously accounts for the full lifetime symptom set, with apparently comorbid features remitting when that condition is treated.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Sequential differential assessment for ADHD, autism, DCD and bipolar-spectrum illness with collateral developmental history, records-based evidence of childhood onset, and structured screening for anxiety, trauma, personality and substance-related contributions.


### H11.1 — ADHD and developmental coordination disorder commonly overlap.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Low
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** In a national cohort at age 12, DCD was diagnosed in 29.4% and ADHD in 21.2% of extremely preterm children (versus 5.7% and 8.9% of term controls), with 59% of those with moderate/severe disability having at least two co-occurring conditions (41638598). In 141 preschoolers with ADHD, parent-rated motor coordination on the DCDQ was significantly associated with ADHD symptom severity (41507887), and DCD appeared alongside ADHD among conditions sharing oculomotor markers of inattention (41121040). (PMID 41638598, PMID 41507887)
- **Strongest opposition:** None of the extracted abstracts reports a pairwise ADHD-and-DCD co-occurrence estimate; the strongest prevalence data come from an extremely preterm cohort in which perinatal injury is a common cause of both. After controlling symptom overlap, hyperactivity-impulsivity was associated with better, not worse, motor coordination (beta = .14), which argues against a uniform ADHD-motor link (41989410). (PMID 41989410)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Motor coordination difficulties and ADHD frequently occur together in high-risk paediatric cohorts and correlate with ADHD symptom severity, but the extracted evidence supplies no direct comorbidity estimate, no adult data, and no performance-based motor testing in ADHD samples. (PMID 41121040)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Shared perinatal or neurological insult causing both conditions in high-risk cohorts; Parent-report halo effects inflating ADHD-motor correlations; Referral bias in clinical samples where multi-domain difficulty prompts assessment; Inattention degrading performance on motor tests without a true motor deficit
- **Methodological weaknesses:** No pairwise ADHD-DCD prevalence estimate available at abstract level; Prevalence data from an extremely preterm rather than general population; Motor coordination assessed by questionnaire rather than standardised performance testing; Paediatric samples only, with no adult comorbidity data
- **Calibrated claim:** ADHD and motor-coordination difficulty co-occur more often than expected in high-risk paediatric cohorts and correlate with ADHD symptom severity, but the extracted abstracts provide no direct ADHD-DCD comorbidity estimate, no adult data, and evidence that the motor association differs by ADHD presentation.
- **Falsifier:** General-population or adult studies using standardised motor performance assessment showing DCD prevalence in ADHD is no higher than in matched controls once perinatal and neurological factors are excluded.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Detailed motor-developmental history with collateral, school records covering handwriting and physical education, birth and perinatal history, and a DCD-focused adult assessment using performance-based motor measures.


### H11.2 — Developmental coordination difficulties can persist into adulthood.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Very Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** The only extracted record measuring adult motor coordination found quantifiable DCD symptomatology on the Adult Developmental Coordination Disorders/Dyspraxia Checklist in 89 community-dwelling midlife adults (mean age 45.6 years), with symptom levels systematically related to sensory over-responsiveness and emotion-regulation difficulties after controlling physical activity (42293966). (PMID 42293966)
- **Strongest opposition:** That evidence is cross-sectional, small, entirely self-report, and drawn from a community sample without childhood motor data or clinical DCD diagnosis, so it cannot demonstrate persistence from childhood. The remaining extracted motor evidence is paediatric (41638598, 41654285, 41507887), and no extracted record follows a childhood-diagnosed DCD cohort into adulthood. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Motor-coordination complaints are measurable and non-trivial in midlife adults using adult-validated self-report checklists, but the extracted evidence contains no longitudinal childhood-to-adult data and therefore supports the presence of adult motor difficulty rather than persistence of a childhood-onset disorder. (PMID 41654285)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Adult-onset or acquired motor difficulty unrelated to childhood DCD; Sensory and emotion-regulation difficulties inflating self-rated motor complaints; Deconditioning and reduced practice accumulating over adulthood; Recall and self-report bias in adults who already suspect a neurodevelopmental explanation
- **Methodological weaknesses:** No longitudinal childhood-to-adult evidence in the extracted set; Single small (n=89) cross-sectional self-report study for the adult claim; No performance-based adult motor testing; Community sample without diagnostic confirmation of DCD
- **Calibrated claim:** Motor-coordination difficulties are measurable in community-dwelling midlife adults using adult self-report instruments, but the extracted evidence includes no longitudinal data and cannot establish that such difficulties persist from childhood-onset DCD.
- **Falsifier:** Longitudinal cohorts following children with confirmed DCD into adulthood that show normalisation of motor performance, or adult studies finding no relationship between documented childhood motor history and adult motor performance.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Childhood motor-milestone and coordination history with family collateral and school records, plus a standardised performance-based adult motor assessment (not self-report alone) repeated during stable mood and medication status.


### H11.3 — Motor impairment can exceed what inattention alone explains.

- **Verdict:** Indeterminate
- **Certainty:** Very Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Low
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** Indirectly, adult motor symptomatology tracked sensory over-responsiveness and emotion-regulation difficulties with physical activity controlled and no attention variable required to explain it (42293966), and in children, objectively measured motor competence predicted fitness independently of perceived competence and activity level (41654285). Neither record, however, tested attention as a competing explanation. (PMID 42293966)
- **Strongest opposition:** The most directly relevant evidence weakens the claim: in a 115-study meta-analysis using regression on pooled correlations, ADHD-inattention symptoms were themselves associated with weaker perceptual-motor functioning (betas -.21 to -.10) while hyperactivity-impulsivity was associated with better motor coordination (beta = .14) (41989410). A transdiagnostic review likewise found candidate oculomotor/motor markers correlated moderately with inattention across conditions including DCD (41121040). (PMID 41989410, PMID 41121040)
- **Qualified interpretation:** No extracted record compared motor impairment with attention statistically or experimentally controlled, and the available overlap-controlled data indicate inattention is itself associated with perceptual-motor weakness, so motor findings cannot be assumed independent of attention. (PMID 41654285)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Inattention degrading performance on attention-demanding motor tasks; Sensory processing and emotion-regulation difficulties driving self-rated motor problems; Reduced practice, deconditioning and low activity exposure; Shared measurement variance when the same informant rates attention and motor skill
- **Methodological weaknesses:** No record directly contrasting motor performance with attention controlled; Reliance on questionnaire rather than performance-based motor measures in ADHD samples; Cross-sectional designs throughout; Motor and attention constructs often measured by the same informant or instrument family
- **Calibrated claim:** Whether motor impairment exceeds what inattention explains is untested in the extracted evidence; the most relevant overlap-controlled meta-analysis shows inattention itself is associated with perceptual-motor weakness, so motor difficulty cannot be presumed independent of attention.
- **Falsifier:** Studies showing motor performance deficits of similar magnitude after statistically controlling attention, and on low-attentional-load motor tasks, would support the claim; their absence or attenuation of deficits under such controls would refute it.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Performance-based adult motor testing paired with concurrent objective attention measures, including tasks with minimal attentional load, conducted during stable medication and mood status, with childhood motor history from collateral sources.


### H11.4 — Neurologic disease, injury, anxiety, medication, obesity, or limited practice can explain motor problems.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** Extreme prematurity was associated with DCD in 29.4% of children versus 5.7% of term controls, demonstrating that perinatal/neurological risk alone can generate high rates of motor disorder (41638598). Latent-profile analysis showed children with low actual but average-or-better perceived motor competence had activity levels comparable to competent peers while fitness remained low, indicating activity and self-perception shape measured motor outcomes (41654285). In midlife adults, emotion-regulation difficulties fully mediated the sensory-over-responsiveness to motor-symptom association with physical activity covaried (42293966). (PMID 41638598, PMID 41654285, PMID 42293966)
- **Strongest opposition:** The extracted evidence tests only some of the listed alternatives: obesity, medication effects, injury and diagnosed neurological disease were not examined in any record. The activity and perceived-competence findings are cross-sectional, so low motor competence may equally reduce practice rather than result from it (41654285), and the mediation analysis rests on concurrent self-report of all constructs (42293966). (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Perinatal risk, emotional-regulation difficulty and physical-activity/self-perception factors are each associated with motor-coordination outcomes and must be excluded before attributing motor difficulty to idiopathic DCD; the specific roles of obesity, medication, injury and neurological disease remain unaddressed by the extracted evidence. (PMID 41121040)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Reverse causation, with poor motor competence reducing practice and fitness; Self-report bias linking distress, sensory sensitivity and motor complaints; Unmeasured perinatal or neurological injury underlying both motor and attention problems; Medication or metabolic effects on coordination not assessed in any extracted record
- **Methodological weaknesses:** No extracted record examined obesity, medication, injury or neurological disease as explanations; Cross-sectional designs preclude causal ordering of activity and motor competence; Self-report measurement of both mediator and outcome in the adult study; High-risk perinatal cohort limits generalisability of the prematurity finding
- **Calibrated claim:** Perinatal risk, emotional-regulation difficulties and physical activity or perceived-competence factors are each associated with motor-coordination outcomes, so these must be excluded before attributing motor difficulty to a neurodevelopmental disorder; obesity, medication, injury and neurological disease were not addressed in the extracted evidence.
- **Falsifier:** Adult studies with performance-based motor assessment showing coordination deficits persist essentially unchanged after full adjustment for BMI, medication, injury history, neurological status, and lifetime physical-activity exposure.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Neurological examination, medication and dose timeline, BMI and lifetime physical-activity/practice history, injury and orthopaedic history, birth and perinatal records, and structured motor-developmental history with collateral.


## Module C: Context, motivation, environmental fit, and proposed strengths

Of 14 abstract-level records, the environmental-fit claims (H6) are best evidenced, but only partially. Accommodation and job redesign are associated with better occupational outcomes in autistic and ADHD adults (41552173, 42297499, 41548993); the ADHD open-plan literature is essentially non-empirical (42132039); and an umbrella review of 134 reviews documents persistent cross-domain adverse outcomes including unemployment (42199907). No included study adjusted for severity, burnout or workload, so those alternatives (H6.4) remain live. Motivational claims (H3) rest on indirect data: elevated novelty seeking with reduced persistence in pediatric ADHD (41085555); an autonomy-plus-feedback package improving motor learning in autistic children (42411300); and exploration-exploitation disturbances across anxiety, mood, OCD, schizophrenia and addiction as well as ADHD (40287643), supporting nonspecificity. Interest and urgency were never experimentally tested. For strengths (H10), hyperfocus was untested; divergent thinking appeared only in a review without a neurodevelopmental sample (41009215); creativity reports were testimonial; and pattern skill was rare, domain-restricted and accompanied by low measured IQ (40972904), with autistic perceptual reasoning near rather than above normative levels (41590022). Relative within-person unevenness is the most defensible strengths finding. Nothing here is diagnostic or treatment guidance.


### H3.1 — Interest can moderate attention or task performance in ADHD.

- **Verdict:** Indeterminate
- **Certainty:** Very Low
- **Evidence directness:** No Eligible Direct Evidence
- **Directness to this case:** Low
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** PMID 42411300, a randomized trial in 30 autistic children, found that a package combining autonomy support (self-controlled practice), enhanced expectancy and external focus improved throwing performance during practice and at 24-h and 72-h retention versus a matched-dose control. (PMID 42411300)
- **Strongest opposition:** No included record manipulated task interest in an ADHD sample. PMID 40287643 characterizes ADHD decision-making as excessive switching and impaired balancing of exploration and exploitation rather than as reliably interest-contingent improvement. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** The available abstract-level evidence supports only that motivational packages containing autonomy support can raise motor-learning performance in autistic children; interest itself was neither isolated nor measured, and no eligible ADHD study tested interest as a moderator. (PMID 40287643)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Effort allocation, practice engagement or adherence rather than interest per se; General motor-learning principles that operate in non-clinical samples; Experimenter attention and expectancy in an unblinded design
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Motivational constructs were not directly measured in the only interventional record; No ADHD sample; extrapolation across diagnoses is unjustified; Small sample (n=30), single task, no blinding reported
- **Calibrated claim:** Whether interest specifically moderates attention or task performance in ADHD is untested in this evidence set; a related motivational package improved motor learning in autistic children.
- **Falsifier:** A within-person crossover in adults with diagnosed ADHD showing equivalent sustained-attention and completion performance on matched high-interest and low-interest tasks, with interest verified by ratings and effort held constant, would materially weaken the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Prospective task-level logs recording task type, rated interest, novelty, urgency, structure, completion, errors and effort, collected during documented euthymia and abstinence, plus collateral ratings to offset self-report.


### H3.2 — Novelty can moderate attention or task performance in ADHD.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Low
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** PMID 41085555 (meta-analysis, 2,525 ADHD youth vs 3,039 controls) reported markedly higher novelty seeking in ADHD (SMD 1.19, 95% CI 0.87-1.51) alongside lower persistence (SMD -1.07, 95% CI -1.33 to -0.81). (PMID 41085555)
- **Strongest opposition:** PMID 40287643 reports that ADHD, alongside schizophrenia and OCD, shows excessive switching and difficulty balancing exploration and exploitation that impairs learning and adaptability, i.e. novelty orientation coupled with worse, not better, task outcomes. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Elevated novelty seeking is a replicated trait-level disposition in ADHD samples, but no included record demonstrates that presenting a novel task improves within-person attention or performance. (PMID 40287643)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Trait novelty seeking is a personality descriptor overlapping ADHD diagnostic items (criterion contamination); Informant bias in parent-rated temperament instruments; Novelty orientation may degrade rather than improve sustained performance
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Pediatric samples (≤18 years); adult generalizability unestablished; Selective reporting of temperament subscales and heterogeneous instruments noted by the authors; Trait questionnaires rather than objective performance under manipulated novelty
- **Calibrated claim:** ADHD samples show elevated self- or informant-rated novelty seeking and reduced persistence; whether novelty improves task performance within individuals remains untested here.
- **Falsifier:** An experimental within-person study in diagnosed adult ADHD showing no performance difference between novel and repeated versions of an otherwise identical task would weaken the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Repeated within-person performance records on matched tasks that differ only in novelty, with mood, sleep and substance status logged concurrently to exclude state confounding.


### H3.3 — Reward and immediate feedback can moderate ADHD task performance.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** PMID 42411300 found that adding positive normative feedback and autonomy support improved performance and retention versus an equal-dose control (24-h retention p=0.019, ηp²=0.18). PMID 42297499 lists structured feedback among interventions reported to reduce occupational injury risk in adults with ADHD. (PMID 42411300, PMID 42297499)
- **Strongest opposition:** PMID 40287643 indicates that reward-related decision-making disturbances in ADHD manifest as impaired adaptability and suboptimal choice, and that similar imbalances occur in anxiety, mood, OCD, schizophrenia and addictive disorders, undercutting a simple reward-improves-performance account. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Feedback and reward manipulations can shift performance in neurodevelopmental samples, but the demonstrations are small, non-ADHD or non-experimental, and reward alterations in ADHD are not uniformly performance-enhancing. (PMID 40287643, PMID 41904958)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Practice, attention from the experimenter, or demand characteristics; Feedback effects that operate identically in non-clinical controls; In occupational data, co-intervention with medication and job reassignment
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Feedback was bundled with autonomy and attentional focus and cannot be isolated; Occupational review reported no risk-of-bias tool and included grey sources; No included study measured reward sensitivity directly alongside performance
- **Calibrated claim:** Reward and structured feedback can alter performance in ADHD and autistic samples, but effects are bundled with other manipulations and are not shown to be diagnosis-specific.
- **Falsifier:** An adequately powered within-person trial in adult ADHD showing no incremental performance benefit from immediate contingent feedback relative to delayed or absent feedback would weaken the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Single-case experimental records alternating immediate feedback and no-feedback conditions on comparable clinical or administrative tasks, changing one variable at a time and reviewed with the treating psychiatrist.


### H3.4 — Urgency or deadlines can alter task initiation or performance.

- **Verdict:** Indeterminate
- **Certainty:** Very Low
- **Evidence directness:** No Eligible Direct Evidence
- **Directness to this case:** Low
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** High
- **Strongest support:** PMID 41904958, a CFIR-guided focus-group needs assessment with 49 stakeholders, found that children and adolescents with ADHD prioritized reminders and prospective-memory support for time-bound daily routines. (None)
- **Strongest opposition:** No included record manipulated deadline proximity or urgency, so no evidence establishes that urgency alters task initiation or performance in either direction. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Only stated preferences for time-related supports are available; these describe perceived need, not measured change in initiation or performance under urgency. (PMID 41904958)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Reported urgency dependence may reflect retrospective narrative reconstruction; Apparent deadline-driven activation may reflect avoidance relief rather than motivational activation; Anxiety escalation near deadlines rather than an ADHD-specific mechanism
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Qualitative preference data with no outcome measurement; Pediatric and caregiver informants; No comparator condition or temporal manipulation
- **Calibrated claim:** No eligible evidence in this module tests whether urgency or deadlines alter task initiation or performance; only stated preferences for time-related supports were identified.
- **Falsifier:** A prospective within-person study showing that initiation latency and completion rates are unchanged across matched tasks with near versus distant deadlines would weaken the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Time-stamped task logs recording deadline distance, initiation latency, completion, error rate and subjective activation, paired with daily sleep and mood charting to separate urgency effects from state effects.


### H3.5 — Motivational effects may be nonspecific rather than unique to ADHD.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** PMID 40287643 (systematic review, 46 studies) reports exploration-exploitation and reward-related decision-making disturbances not only in ADHD but also in anxiety, mood, obsessive-compulsive, schizophrenia and addictive disorders, indicating that motivational alterations are transdiagnostic rather than ADHD-specific. (PMID 40287643, PMID 42411300)
- **Strongest opposition:** PMID 41085555 shows large effect sizes distinguishing ADHD from controls on novelty seeking and persistence, which is compatible with some degree of ADHD-linked motivational patterning rather than pure nonspecificity. (PMID 41085555)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Motivational and reward-related alterations appear across multiple psychiatric conditions, so a motivation-sensitive performance pattern cannot by itself discriminate ADHD from mood, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive or substance-related explanations. (None)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Shared transdiagnostic dopaminergic/noradrenergic mechanisms; Comorbidity within ADHD samples inflating apparent specificity; Task-general motivational effects present in neurotypical controls
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Narrative synthesis of heterogeneous tasks with a single-database search; Case-control designs cannot establish diagnostic specificity; Few studies directly compare diagnostic groups on identical motivational paradigms
- **Calibrated claim:** Motivational modulation of performance is documented across several psychiatric conditions and should not be treated as evidence for ADHD specifically without direct differential comparison.
- **Falsifier:** Head-to-head studies showing that interest-, novelty- or reward-contingent performance gains are significantly larger in ADHD than in anxiety, mood and substance-use comparison groups on identical tasks would weaken the counterclaim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Prospective task records collected during documented euthymia and abstinence, plus structured differential assessment for mood, anxiety and substance effects, to test whether motivational patterning persists independent of state.


### H6.1 — Person-environment fit can moderate neurodevelopmental impairment.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** PMID 41552173 (systematic review of 10 studies, 2010-2025) reports that workplace accommodations spanning assistive technology, organizational/sensory/environmental adaptation, supervisory support and psychosocial support were associated with better job acquisition, stability, satisfaction and productivity in autistic adults. (PMID 41552173, PMID 42297499, PMID 41548993)
- **Strongest opposition:** PMID 42199907, an umbrella review of 134 reviews, documents pervasive adverse outcomes in autistic people across mental health, physical health and social domains including unemployment and reduced quality of life, indicating substantial burden that environmental modification alone has not been shown to remove. (PMID 42199907)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Accommodation and environmental modification are consistently associated with better occupational outcomes, but the evidence is observational, self-report heavy, and does not establish that fit moderates underlying impairment rather than co-varying with disclosure, severity and organizational culture. (PMID 42132039, PMID 40665716, PMID 41943443)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Selection: workers who obtain accommodations may already have milder impairment or better supports; Disclosure and supervisory relationship quality as the operative variable; Reverse causation, with better functioning enabling accommodation negotiation
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Small samples and reliance on self-report acknowledged by review authors; No within-person environmental manipulation with objective outcomes; Heterogeneous accommodation definitions preclude pooling
- **Calibrated claim:** Workplace accommodation and environmental modification are associated with improved occupational outcomes in autistic and ADHD adults; a moderating effect on underlying impairment is plausible but not demonstrated.
- **Falsifier:** A prospective within-person study showing equivalent impairment and functional outcomes across substantially different, well-characterized work environments after adjustment for workload, mood and symptom severity would weaken the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Prospective, setting-stratified impairment ratings across genuinely different work contexts, with concurrent workload, sleep, mood and conflict logs, plus supervisor or collateral ratings alongside self-report.


### H6.2 — Autonomy, meaning, or task fit can improve functioning.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** PMID 42411300 randomized autistic children to an autonomy-supportive protocol and found better performance and retention than an equal-dose control; PMID 41552173 found individually tailored support and respectful supervisory relationships to be consistent correlates of better employment outcomes. (PMID 42411300, PMID 41552173)
- **Strongest opposition:** PMID 41943443 documents highly heterogeneous vocational interventions with variable dose (median 23 weeks, 32 contact hours) and inconsistent outcome measurement, so autonomy and fit cannot be separated from intervention intensity or setting. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Autonomy support has experimental backing in one small pediatric motor-learning trial and observational backing in employment reviews, but meaning and task fit have not been isolated as active ingredients in adult occupational outcomes. (PMID 41943443, PMID 41904958, PMID 41548993)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Intensity, duration and staff contact rather than autonomy per se; Selection into autonomous roles by higher-functioning individuals; Expectancy and preference-matching effects in unblinded designs
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Bundled manipulations preclude component isolation; Preference data (PMID 41904958) reflect stated need, not measured outcome; Occupational evidence is observational with heterogeneous outcome instruments
- **Calibrated claim:** Autonomy support can improve learning in a small autistic pediatric trial and is associated with better employment outcomes; meaning and task fit remain plausible but empirically unseparated contributors.
- **Falsifier:** A trial isolating autonomy support in adults with ADHD or autism that shows no benefit over a matched-dose controlled-instruction condition would weaken the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Repeated measures of completion, errors and effort before and after introducing one autonomy- or meaning-related change at a time in the actual work role, with collateral observation.


### H6.3 — Standardized or repetitive demands can amplify impairment.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** PMID 41548993 reports that neurodivergent surgical trainees described difficulties with assessments, sensory environments and professional expectations, with lower pass rates in early standardized postgraduate examinations. PMID 42297499 found the strongest ADHD-injury associations in highly proceduralized industries such as manufacturing and construction. (PMID 41548993, PMID 42297499)

(PMID 41552173, PMID 40665716)

### H6.4 — Severity, burnout, workload, or interpersonal conflict may explain apparent mismatch effects.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** High
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** PMID 41552173 found that accommodation effectiveness varied by relational quality, disclosure practice and organizational culture, and PMID 42199907 documents broad cross-domain adverse outcomes, both consistent with severity, interpersonal and organizational factors rather than environmental fit alone driving observed differences. (PMID 41552173, PMID 42199907, PMID 40665716)

- **Methodological weaknesses:** No included study adjusted for burnout or workload; Qualitative and scoping designs cannot partition variance; Common-method bias where exposure and outcome are both self-reported
- **Calibrated claim:** Severity, burnout, workload and interpersonal conflict are plausible and untested alternative explanations for apparent environmental-mismatch effects in this evidence set.

### H10.5 — Proposed strengths and impairments can represent context-dependent trade-offs.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** PMID 41590022 (meta-analysis, 31 studies) found a distinct ASD profile in which Processing Speed and Working Memory indices were markedly impaired while Verbal Comprehension, Perceptual/Fluid Reasoning and Visual Processing remained close to normative levels, demonstrating within-person unevenness rather than uniform deficit. (PMID 41590022, PMID 40972904)
- **Strongest opposition:** The same analysis showed generalized deficits across all indices in ID and ASD+ID with no comparable sparing, and PMID 40972904 shows that savant-level skill co-occurs with low overall measured IQ, so unevenness is not a general rule and the sparing is relative, not superior. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Uneven cognitive profiles with relatively preserved reasoning alongside impaired processing speed and working memory are documented in ASD; this supports relative, context-dependent trade-offs rather than absolute strengths. (None)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Test-construction artefacts in timed versus untimed subtests; Comorbid ID or language level driving profile shape; Referral and ascertainment bias in clinically assessed samples
- **Methodological weaknesses:** High heterogeneity across ASD and ID subgroups; Cross-sectional psychometric data cannot establish contextual trade-off; No occupational or real-world outcome linked to the profile
- **Calibrated claim:** Autistic samples show uneven cognitive profiles with relatively preserved reasoning alongside impaired processing speed and working memory; this is a relative, not absolute, strength-impairment pattern.
- **Falsifier:** Meta-analytic evidence showing uniformly depressed indices in ASD without differential sparing of verbal or perceptual reasoning would weaken the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Full-index neuropsychological assessment with subtest-level scatter analysis, paired with real-world completion and error data, to test whether measured unevenness maps onto observed functional trade-offs.


### H10.6 — Education, intelligence, personality, mood, or selection bias can explain proposed strengths.

- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** High
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** PMID 40972904 illustrates strong selection effects in the savant literature (105 calculators, 95 male, case-based across a century) with generally low measured IQ; PMID 41590022 shows that apparent strengths in ASD correspond to measured cognitive indices; PMID 41085555 shows that personality dimensions such as novelty seeking differ substantially in ADHD samples. (PMID 40972904, PMID 41590022, PMID 41085555, PMID 41009215, PMID 42199907)

(None)

## Module D: External cognitive scaffolding

Across 14 abstract-level records, the strongest evidence for external scaffolding in ADHD concerns structured psychological and organizational interventions: meta-analytic organizational skills training improves rated organizational behaviour in children and adolescents, adolescent psychosocial packages most robustly improve concrete behaviours such as homework completion and planner use, and a large adult meta-analysis reports moderate CBT effects on global and especially occupational functioning. Evidence is weaker and more contested for digital aids: a pediatric network meta-analysis found symptom improvement but a non-significant executive-function effect, a youth web/app meta-analysis was pooled-null with high heterogeneity, and an umbrella review judged effectiveness inconclusive while documenting inconsistently reported adverse effects. Occupational or educational redesign is supported only indirectly through school-based trials and qualitative adult accounts; no eligible record tested adult workplace redesign. AI-assisted organization has no eligible evidence of durable clinical benefit, with general AI mental-health syntheses showing short follow-up, attenuation on external validation, and incomplete governance. Durability, burden, and harm qualifications are substantiated, notably loss of CBT-plus-medication advantage by 6-9 months and non-significant quality-of-life effects at follow-up. Findings are group-level, largely pediatric or self-report based, and carry no diagnostic or treatment implication for any individual.


### H4.1 — Checklists and reminders can improve real-world task performance.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** Meta-analytic organizational skills training in ADHD produced moderate-to-large gains in rated organizational skills (teacher g=0.54, 95% CI 0.17-0.91; parent g=0.83, 0.32-1.34) with smaller academic gains (PMID 28088557), and an adolescent systematic review found psychosocial packages produced their most robust effects on concrete organizational behaviours such as homework completion and planner use (PMID 27163988). Adults with ADHD qualitatively describe reminders and working within imposed structure as key protective strategies (PMID 28398986). (PMID 28088557, PMID 27163988, PMID 28398986)
- **Strongest opposition:** In a mixed-methods review of ADHD adherence interventions, qualitative comparative analysis identified effective configurations characterized by education or tracking together with the absence of reminders, indicating reminders are not necessarily the active component (PMID 38006238). (PMID 38006238)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Structured organizational scaffolding reliably improves rated organizational behaviours in children and adolescents; isolated checklists or reminders alone have not been shown to be the causal ingredient, and adult objective task-completion data are lacking in these abstracts. (PMID 38006238)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Expectancy and reporter bias in unblinded parent/teacher ratings; Nonspecific effects of therapist contact, monitoring, and attention; Concurrent medication or maturation effects; Self-selection of motivated participants and families
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Pediatric samples predominate; adult evidence is qualitative; Outcomes are rating-scale based rather than objective task logs; Parent-rated effects consistently exceed teacher-rated effects; No durability or harms data reported in abstracts
- **Calibrated claim:** Structured organizational-skills scaffolding improves rated organizational behaviour in children and adolescents with ADHD, and adults report reminders and external structure as helpful; evidence that checklists or reminders alone improve objectively measured adult real-world performance remains weak.
- **Falsifier:** Adequately powered adult trials or within-person A-B-A designs showing no improvement in objectively logged task completion or omissions when checklist/reminder scaffolds are added and withdrawn, with monitoring intensity held constant, would materially weaken the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Prospective task-level logs (task type, structure, urgency, completion, errors, effort) collected before, during, and after introducing a single scaffold at a time, with collateral or manager verification, and mood/sleep state recorded to control for confounding.


### H4.2 — ADHD coaching or executive-function-focused CBT can improve functioning.

- **Verdict:** Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** Direct
- **Directness to this case:** High
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** A comprehensive meta-analysis of 70 studies in 5152 adults with ADHD found CBT produced moderate improvement in global functioning (SMD=0.47) increasing at follow-up (SMD=0.67), with strongest effects for occupational functioning (PMID 41936228); a systematic review of 19 adult RCTs found behavioral therapy reduced inattention (SMD range 0.44-1.71) while neurofeedback, cognitive training, and psychoeducation did not (PMID 32954909). (PMID 41936228, PMID 32954909, PMID 28088557, PMID 27163988)
- **Strongest opposition:** Adding CBT to medication was superior to medication alone only at 3 months, with no significant difference at 6 and 9 months (PMID 38084075); in the same large meta-analysis quality-of-life effects were smaller and non-significant at follow-up (PMID 41936228), and higher-education support provision for neurodivergent students is fragmented, rarely wellbeing-focused, and under-evaluated (PMID 41741588). (PMID 38084075)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Executive-function-focused CBT and structured behavioral therapy improve adult ADHD functioning with moderate effect sizes, but benefits are modality-specific, may require continued input, and do not reliably transfer to quality of life; formal ADHD coaching specifically remains sparsely evidenced. (PMID 38084075, PMID 41741588, PMID 41936228)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Nonspecific therapeutic contact, structure, and monitoring effects; Concurrent pharmacotherapy or dose optimization; Regression to the mean after help-seeking at peak impairment; Self-report outcome inflation in unblinded participants
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Inclusion of single-arm and single-case designs alongside RCTs; Reliance on self-report functional outcomes; limited blinded or collateral ratings; Typically 3-month follow-ups; sparse long-term durability data; Western sample concentration and unexplained country-level moderation
- **Calibrated claim:** CBT and structured behavioral therapy for adult ADHD are associated with moderate improvements in functioning, particularly occupational functioning, with limited and possibly attenuating durability and no reliable quality-of-life gain; coaching specifically lacks comparable adult trial evidence.
- **Falsifier:** Adequately powered, blinded-rater adult RCTs with 12-month follow-up showing no advantage of executive-function-focused CBT over an active, equally structured control on functional outcomes would weaken the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Baseline and repeated standardized adult ADHD impairment ratings with collateral informants before and after a defined CBT or coaching course, with a documented maintenance phase to test durability, alongside a medication and mood-state timeline.


### H4.3 — Digital aids or assistive technology can support executive functioning.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Low
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** A network meta-analysis of 37 pediatric ADHD RCTs found digital interventions improved inattention (SMD=-0.44, 95% CI -0.62 to -0.26) and hyperactivity-impulsivity (SMD=-0.26, -0.41 to -0.12) (PMID 42358407). (PMID 42358407)
- **Strongest opposition:** In the same analysis the pooled executive-function effect was not significant (SMD=-0.41, 95% CI -0.85 to 0.02) (PMID 42358407); a meta-analysis of web/app interventions in youth neurodevelopmental disorders found a non-significant pooled effect with high heterogeneity (SMD=-0.39, 95% CI -0.98 to 0.20; I²=72%), with all four null trials app-delivered (PMID 31682573); and an umbrella review of 26 reviews judged digital ADHD intervention effectiveness inconclusive on low-quality evidence with inconsistently reported harms (PMID 40264083). (PMID 31682573, PMID 40264083)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Digital tools can reduce rated ADHD symptoms in children and adolescents, but effects on measured executive functioning are unproven, app-only delivery has frequently been null, and adult digital-support literature is predominantly preliminary and non-functional in focus (PMID 41808914). (PMID 42358407, PMID 41808914)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Symptom rating improvements driven by engagement, novelty, or expectancy rather than executive change; Practice effects on trained tasks without far transfer; Heterogeneous inactive controls inflating apparent benefit; Publication and small-study bias in a fragmented literature
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Pediatric samples dominate; few adult functional trials; No real-world task-completion or omission outcomes; High heterogeneity and underpowered pooled analyses; Harms and dependence inconsistently measured
- **Calibrated claim:** Digital aids and assistive technologies show small-to-moderate effects on rated ADHD symptoms in youth, but there is currently insufficient evidence that they durably improve measured executive functioning or adult real-world task performance.
- **Falsifier:** Adequately powered adult RCTs of digital organizational aids with objective real-world completion/omission endpoints and active control conditions showing no benefit, or consistent evidence of attenuation on withdrawal, would weaken the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Time-stamped digital task records (planned vs completed vs missed tasks, errors) during defined periods of digital-aid use and non-use, with engagement metrics and concurrent sleep, mood, and substance logs.


### H4.4 — Occupational or educational redesign can reduce functional impairment.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Low
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** A meta-analysis of 22 school-based ADHD RCTs (n=1962) found improvements in combined ADHD symptoms (d=-0.28), inattention (d=-0.33), academic performance (d=0.37), social skills (d=0.28), and externalising problems (d=-0.32) (PMID 40761448); adults with ADHD qualitatively report that supervisor-provided task structure and clear rules support workplace functioning (PMID 28398986). (PMID 40761448, PMID 28398986)
- **Strongest opposition:** The same school-based synthesis found no significant effect on hyperactivity/impulsivity (d=-0.09, p=.22) and acknowledged heterogeneity and reporter bias (PMID 40761448); higher-education support for neurodivergent students is heterogeneous, largely limited to academic adjustments rather than wellbeing, and rarely co-designed or rigorously evaluated (PMID 41741588). (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Modifying educational environments and supports is associated with reduced impairment in some domains for young people with ADHD, but no eligible abstract here tested adult occupational redesign or job crafting, so the workplace-level claim is extrapolation. (PMID 41741588, PMID 40761448)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Improvement attributable to added adult attention and monitoring rather than environmental redesign per se; Concurrent medication, maturation, or teacher expectancy effects; Selection of schools and institutions already better resourced; Domain-specific measurement artefacts in rater-based outcomes
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Search restricted to three journals in the school-based meta-analysis; No adult occupational-redesign trials in this evidence set; Narrative synthesis without effect estimates in the higher-education review; Absence of objective performance and burnout outcomes
- **Calibrated claim:** Educational environment modification and school-based support reduce some ADHD-related impairments in young people; evidence that adult occupational redesign or job crafting reduces functional impairment is currently absent from this evidence set and remains an untested extrapolation.
- **Falsifier:** Controlled adult workplace-redesign or job-crafting trials in ADHD showing no reduction in impairment after accounting for workload, mood, and interpersonal conflict would materially weaken the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Structured comparison of impairment ratings, error/omission rates, and burnout measures across documented periods of differing autonomy, novelty, standardization, and workload, with workload hours and mood/sleep state recorded concurrently.


### H4.5 — AI-assisted organization has evidence for durable clinical benefit.

- **Verdict:** Unsupported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Highly Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Low
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** No eligible abstract demonstrated durable clinical benefit of AI-assisted organization; the closest signals are short-term small-to-moderate symptom effects from conversational agents in general mental health populations (PMID 41853071). (None)
- **Strongest opposition:** A scoping review of 31 reviews found AI mental-health applications remain largely proof-of-concept with performance attenuating on external validation, few randomized or pragmatic chatbot trials, and limited data on durability beyond 12 weeks (PMID 41853071); a scoping review of 133 adult ADHD digital health technology studies found the field dominated by preliminary efficacy and classification work with unclear clinical integration and little demonstrated value in remote monitoring (PMID 41808914). (PMID 41853071, PMID 41808914)
- **Qualified interpretation:** AI-assisted organizational support for adult ADHD is an emerging, largely untested application: there is no eligible evidence of durable clinical benefit, and general AI mental-health evidence is short-term, heterogeneous, and weakly governed. (PMID 41808914)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Short-term engagement, novelty, and expectancy effects mistaken for durable benefit; Benefit attributable to the act of externalizing and structuring tasks rather than the AI component; Publication bias toward positive proof-of-concept reports; Self-report improvement without objective performance change
- **Methodological weaknesses:** No separable ADHD or executive-dysfunction population in the AI evidence; No organizational or task-completion outcomes; Follow-up rarely exceeds 12 weeks; Scoping designs preclude pooled effect estimation
- **Calibrated claim:** There is currently no eligible evidence that AI-assisted organization produces durable clinical benefit in adults with ADHD; existing AI mental-health evidence is short-term, indirect, and not focused on executive or organizational functioning.
- **Falsifier:** A well-conducted randomized trial in adults with ADHD showing AI-assisted organizational support outperforming an active non-AI structuring control on objective functional outcomes maintained at 6-12 months would support the claim as written.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Prospective, single-scaffold-at-a-time records of AI-assisted planning use versus a comparably structured non-AI method, with objective completion/omission counts, sustained across at least several months of stable mood, sleep, and medication.


### H4.6 — External scaffolds can have limited durability, dependence, burden, or harms.

- **Verdict:** Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** CBT added to medication lost its advantage by 6-9 months (PMID 38084075); quality-of-life effects were non-significant at follow-up in the largest adult CBT meta-analysis (PMID 41936228); an umbrella review documented adverse effects including physical discomfort, emotional reactions, and video-game addiction in 8 of 26 digital-intervention reviews, with inconsistent harms reporting (PMID 40264083); pooled web/app effects were non-significant (PMID 31682573); executive-function effects of digital interventions were non-significant (PMID 42358407); reminders appeared in ineffective rather than effective adherence configurations (PMID 38006238); AI-based supports lack durability data beyond 12 weeks (PMID 41853071); and adult ADHD digital technologies show limited demonstrated adherence value and unclear implementation (PMID 41808914). (PMID 38084075, PMID 40264083, PMID 31682573, PMID 42358407, PMID 38006238, PMID 41853071, PMID 41808914, PMID 41936228)
- **Strongest opposition:** Some benefits appear maintained or growing: adult CBT global functioning effects increased from SMD=0.47 post-treatment to 0.67 at follow-up (PMID 41936228), and behavioral therapy showed sizeable inattention effects in adults (PMID 32954909), arguing against uniform decay. (PMID 32954909)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Durability, burden, and harm limitations are real but heterogeneous: therapy-based scaffolds may show maintained functional gains in directly targeted domains, whereas medication-combination advantages, quality-of-life effects, and digital tool benefits show attenuation, inconsistency, or documented adverse effects; formal dependence has not been directly measured. (PMID 41936228, PMID 41741588, PMID 40761448)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Attrition and selective follow-up reporting creating apparent decay or apparent maintenance; Discontinuation of the scaffold rather than intrinsic loss of effect; Outcome-domain mismatch (symptoms versus functioning versus quality of life); Harms under-ascertained because most trials do not systematically collect them
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Harms and dependence rarely predefined or standardized outcomes; Short follow-ups (commonly ~3 months) across the evidence base; Heterogeneous interventions pooled or narratively combined; Predominantly pediatric samples for digital and school-based evidence
- **Calibrated claim:** External scaffolds show inconsistent durability, incomplete harms reporting, and documented adverse effects in some digital modalities; evidence for true dependence is not directly measured, and some therapy-based functional gains appear maintained at short follow-up.
- **Falsifier:** Long-term trials with prespecified harms, burden, and post-withdrawal outcomes showing maintained benefit after scaffold discontinuation and no meaningful adverse effects would weaken this qualification.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Planned scaffold-withdrawal and reintroduction periods (reviewed with the treating psychiatrist), with objective completion/omission data, subjective burden and reliance ratings, and adverse-effect logging including screen-time and sleep displacement.


## Module E1: Sleep and circadian modifiers

Across 14 abstract-level records, sleep and circadian disturbance were consistently associated with attentional, executive, mood-instability and mania-related outcomes in ADHD and bipolar-spectrum populations, but the strength and direction of these associations varied substantially. The most direct adult evidence (actigraphy in ADHD and euthymic bipolar disorder, six-month daily smartphone monitoring in 370 bipolar patients) showed small-to-moderate correlations, sometimes fragile after multiplicity correction and sometimes in unexpected directions (longer sleep also associated with poorer cognition and with progression to bipolar or psychotic disorders). Prospective and multimodal work linked rest-activity instability to later mania symptoms, while a 10-week actigraphy study found mood instability unrelated to circadian indices and a genetic analysis found no causal sleep effect on postpartum psychosis, with insomnia liability appearing protective. Longitudinal cohort and pharmaco-epidemiological data indicate substantial bidirectionality: ADHD symptoms predicted later sleep disturbance, medication class strongly shaped sleep outcomes, and bipolar sleep profiles differed by mood phase. No eligible empirical shift-work study was retrieved, leaving that sub-claim untested. Overall, sleep and circadian state are credible modifiers within a plural formulation, but the literature does not license one-directional causal statements and cannot be applied diagnostically to any individual.


### H7.1 — Short or inadequate sleep can worsen attention and executive functioning.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** In adults with ADHD, longer objectively measured sleep latency and shorter total sleep time were associated with poorer selective attention and inhibitory control (41803808); in euthymic bipolar adults, actigraphic sleep duration, latency and WASO were associated with poorer SCIP cognitive scores (42371207); in a large adolescent cohort, shorter Fitbit-measured sleep duration was associated with attention symptoms cross-sectionally and one year later (41870441). (PMID 41803808, PMID 42371207, PMID 41870441)
- **Strongest opposition:** No eligible experimental sleep-deprivation study was available; in adult ADHD most sleep-cognition correlations did not survive multiple-comparison correction (41803808), and adolescent effect sizes were small (β -0.06 to -0.11) with irregularity effects not persisting prospectively (41870441). (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Inadequate or disturbed sleep is consistently but weakly associated with poorer attention and executive performance in ADHD and bipolar samples; associations are small, largely correlational and not uniformly in the 'shorter sleep is worse' direction (42371207 found longer sleep also associated with poorer cognition). (PMID 42192834)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Mood, anxiety or hyperarousal states degrading both sleep and cognition; Medication effects on sleep and on test performance; Reverse direction: attentional/executive difficulty producing irregular sleep behaviour; Effort, motivation and testing-condition variability in laboratory cognitive tasks
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Cross-sectional designs dominate; no eligible experimental sleep restriction study; Multiplicity and fragile significance after correction; Laboratory cognitive tasks may not index real-world functioning; Small samples with no control group in the bipolar actigraphy study
- **Calibrated claim:** Short or disturbed sleep shows small, mostly cross-sectional associations with poorer attention and executive performance in adults with ADHD or bipolar disorder; the evidence available here is insufficient to quantify how much sleep loss worsens real-world executive functioning in any individual.
- **Falsifier:** Adequately powered within-person experimental or prospective studies in adults with ADHD showing no deterioration in attention, inhibition or real-world task completion after documented sleep restriction, with covariate control for mood and medication.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Prospective daily sleep logs plus actigraphy paired with repeated objective attention/executive testing and task-level completion records during euthymia and abstinence, ideally with sleep varying naturally rather than by intentional deprivation.


### H7.2 — Sleep quality and circadian irregularity can worsen ADHD-related functioning.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Direct
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** Adults with ADHD showed more unstable and delayed rest-activity rhythms than controls, with sleep parameters relating to attention and inhibition (41803808); poor sleep quality and insomnia were highly prevalent in bipolar disorder across phases including euthymia (40706099, 42512516); daily sleep variation tracked mood instability in 370 bipolar patients (42320790); and genetically proxied sleep traits were linked to ADHD and bipolar liability (41517697). (PMID 41803808, PMID 40706099, PMID 42512516, PMID 42320790, PMID 41517697, PMID 42371207, PMID 41870441)
- **Strongest opposition:** ADHD symptoms predicted later sleep disturbance with a lag effect and ADHD medication weakened the ADHD-sleep association (42297780); medication and comorbidity, rather than circadian traits, predicted sleep outcomes in a prospective adult ADHD cohort where chronotype predicted nothing (41397339); and mood instability was unrelated to objective circadian indices in bipolar-risk adults (42464599). (PMID 42297780, PMID 42464599)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Poor sleep quality and irregular or delayed circadian patterns co-occur robustly with ADHD- and bipolar-related impairment, but the eligible evidence is largely cross-sectional or bidirectional and does not establish that circadian irregularity is the driver rather than a correlate or consequence. (PMID 41397339, PMID 42192834)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** ADHD or mood symptom severity generating irregular schedules (reverse causation); Medication (stimulants, antidepressants) altering sleep architecture and timing; Comorbid anxiety, depression, obesity or sleep-disordered breathing; Common-method bias when both sleep and functioning are self-reported
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Predominance of cross-sectional and self-report designs; Confounding by indication for medication exposures; Chronotype and circadian indices often unassociated with outcomes after adjustment; Heterogeneous sleep instruments across studies
- **Calibrated claim:** Sleep quality problems and circadian irregularity are strongly associated with worse functioning in ADHD and bipolar-spectrum populations, but current evidence supports a bidirectional relationship rather than a demonstrated one-way worsening effect.
- **Falsifier:** Prospective within-person adult data showing that periods of poorer sleep quality or greater circadian irregularity are not followed by worse ADHD-related functioning once mood state, medication and workload are accounted for.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Concurrent actigraphy or wearable sleep-timing data, standardized sleep-quality and chronotype measures, medication timeline, and prospective ratings of ADHD-related functioning across settings, analysed within-person.

- **Certainty:** Very Low
- **Evidence directness:** Highly Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Low
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** A narrative review of Daylight Saving Time reported that externally imposed circadian misalignment is associated with sleep disturbance, impaired executive functioning, heightened emotional reactivity, depressive symptoms and elevated manic-episode risk in people with chronic mental illness (42192834). (None)
- **Strongest opposition:** No eligible empirical study of shift work with ADHD or bipolar-specific cognitive, mood or functional outcomes was identified in this abstract-level set; the supporting source is a non-systematic narrative review without effect estimates. (None)

(PMID 42192834)

- **Methodological weaknesses:** No eligible empirical shift-work study retrieved; Reliance on a narrative review without systematic search or bias appraisal; Extrapolation from time-transition studies to sustained shift schedules
- **Calibrated claim:** Shift work is widely presumed to impair sleep, cognition and mood, but this rapid abstract-level review identified no direct quantified evidence in ADHD or bipolar-spectrum samples, so the claim remains untested here.
- **Falsifier:** A well-conducted cohort or within-person study of shift-working adults with ADHD or bipolar disorder showing no deterioration in sleep, cognition or mood relative to day-work periods after adjusting for workload and sleep opportunity.

### H7.4 — Within-person symptom variation can track sleep changes.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Direct
- **Directness to this case:** High
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** In 370 bipolar patients providing six months of daily ratings, short-term sleep variation was strongly associated with mood instability (42320790); rest-activity instability predicted mania symptoms six months later in vulnerable youth (41646692); and objectively measured sleep duration and irregularity related to symptoms cross-sectionally and prospectively in adolescents (41870441). (PMID 42320790, PMID 41646692, PMID 41870441)
- **Strongest opposition:** Mood and subjective-state instability were unrelated to actigraphic circadian indices in bipolar-risk adults over 10 weeks (42464599); adolescent sleep irregularity effects did not persist prospectively (41870441); and ADHD symptoms predicted later sleep disturbance rather than the reverse (42297780). (PMID 42464599, PMID 42297780)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Within-person symptom variation does co-vary with sleep changes in several datasets, but the strongest association comes from wholly self-reported data with unresolved temporal ordering, and one objective study found no circadian-instability link, so co-variation is better established than direction. (PMID 41822684, PMID 41397339)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Mood or arousal state altering both perceived sleep and symptom reporting; Recall and common-method bias in daily self-report; Behavioural or environmental drivers (workload, social schedule) affecting sleep and symptoms simultaneously; Medication changes coinciding with sleep shifts
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Self-reported exposure and outcome in the largest within-person dataset; Post hoc exploratory analyses without prespecified temporal models; Null objective findings in a smaller prospective study; Different sleep constructs across studies limit comparability
- **Calibrated claim:** Within-person fluctuations in sleep and in symptoms are reliably correlated in bipolar-spectrum samples, but available evidence does not establish which changes first, and one objective study found no association with circadian indices.
- **Falsifier:** Prospective within-person data with objective sleep measurement and blinded or clinician-rated symptoms showing no lagged association in either direction, or showing symptom change consistently preceding sleep change.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Daily mood charting (sleep duration/quality, energy, irritability, speech pressure, impulsivity, unusual certainty) combined with actigraphy over months, with lagged within-person analysis and collateral observation, plus a documented medication and substance timeline.


### H7.5 — Sleep loss can precipitate or amplify bipolar-spectrum mood symptoms.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** Rest-activity instability predicted mania symptoms six months later and was mediated by white matter organisation in mania-vulnerable youth (41646692); drug-free manic/mixed states showed shorter sleep time, lower efficiency, shortened REM latency and increased REM density relative to controls and depressive phases (42512516, 40706099); shorter and more irregular adolescent sleep related prospectively to mania and psychotic-like symptoms (41870441). (PMID 41646692, PMID 42512516, PMID 40706099, PMID 41870441, PMID 41517697)
- **Strongest opposition:** Mendelian randomisation did not support a causal effect of sleep traits on postpartum psychosis, and higher genetic liability to insomnia or short sleep was associated with roughly halved risk (41644956); in a youth clinical cohort it was longer, not shorter, baseline sleep that predicted transition to bipolar or psychotic disorders (41822684). (PMID 41644956, PMID 41822684)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Sleep and circadian disturbance are tightly coupled to bipolar mood states and forecast mania symptoms in some prospective datasets, but the eligible evidence cannot separate sleep loss as a precipitant from reduced sleep need as an early manic symptom, and genetic analyses do not confirm a causal sleep-to-mania pathway. (PMID 42192834)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Reduced sleep as an intrinsic prodromal symptom of hypomania rather than its cause; Substance exposure (cannabis, stimulants, nicotine) driving both sleep loss and activation; Medication changes or nonadherence preceding both sleep change and relapse; Life-event stress or workload disrupting sleep and mood concurrently
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Case-control and cross-sectional designs measuring sleep concurrently with mood state; Genetic proxies do not measure actual sleep deprivation; Directionally inconsistent findings (short versus long sleep as predictor); Symptom-level rather than diagnosed-episode outcomes in cohort studies
- **Calibrated claim:** Sleep and circadian disturbance are consistently associated with, and in some prospective data precede, bipolar-spectrum mood symptoms, but current evidence does not establish that sleep loss itself reliably precipitates mania rather than being an early manifestation of it.
- **Falsifier:** Well-controlled prospective or genetically informed studies showing no temporal or causal relationship between documented sleep loss and subsequent hypomanic/manic symptom emergence after excluding prodromal reduced sleep need, substances and medication changes.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Longitudinal mood charting with objective sleep duration, plus a dated substance and medication timeline and collateral observations, examined for whether sleep reduction precedes or accompanies episodes of elevated salience, pressured speech or unusual certainty; intentional sleep deprivation should not be attempted.


### H7.N1 — Some apparent sleep effects may reflect reverse causation from mood or baseline severity.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Direct
- **Directness to this case:** High
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** ADHD symptoms predicted later sleep disturbance with a lag effect, and ADHD medication attenuated the ADHD-sleep association (42297780); medication class, comorbidity, sex and BMI—not chronotype—predicted sleep outcomes in a prospective adult ADHD cohort (41397339); mood phase itself determined polysomnographic sleep profile in bipolar disorder (42512516, 40706099); mood instability was unrelated to objective circadian indices (42464599); and Mendelian randomisation did not support sleep traits causing postpartum psychosis (41644956). (PMID 42297780, PMID 41397339, PMID 42512516, PMID 40706099, PMID 42464599, PMID 41644956)
- **Strongest opposition:** Genetically proxied sleep traits were associated with ADHD and bipolar liability in an MR synthesis, arguing that at least part of the sleep-psychopathology link is not purely reverse causation (41517697); prospective rest-activity instability preceded mania symptoms (41646692) and objective sleep measures preceded symptom change in adolescents (41870441). (PMID 41517697, PMID 41646692, PMID 41870441)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Reverse causation and confounding by mood state, baseline severity and medication plausibly account for a substantial portion of observed sleep-symptom associations, but prospective and genetic evidence indicates the relationship is bidirectional rather than exclusively reverse. (PMID 42320790, PMID 41822684, PMID 41803808)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Bidirectional feedback between sleep and symptom severity; Shared genetic and neurobiological liability rather than either causal direction; Medication and substance effects generating spurious sleep-symptom coupling; Common-method and recall bias inflating self-reported associations
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Few studies model temporal direction explicitly; Concurrent measurement of sleep and mood state in case-control designs; Medication exposures confounded by indication; Small samples and low precision in the null objective studies
- **Calibrated claim:** Part of the apparent effect of sleep on symptoms is plausibly attributable to reverse causation, mood state, baseline severity and medication, but evidence indicates a bidirectional relationship rather than reverse causation alone.
- **Falsifier:** Prospective within-person or genetically informed analyses in which sleep change consistently precedes and predicts symptom change after full adjustment for prior symptom severity, mood state, medication and substance use, leaving no residual reverse pathway.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Lagged within-person analysis of objectively measured sleep against clinician- and collateral-rated symptoms during periods of stable medication and abstinence, plus records distinguishing reduced sleep opportunity from reduced sleep need.


## Module E2: Mood, substances, and medication state effects

Stimulant evidence is the most quantitative: prescription amphetamines showed a dose-graded association with incident psychosis or mania (adjusted OR 2.68; 5.28-fold above 30 mg dextroamphetamine equivalents) with a null methylphenidate result, and pooled occurrences of roughly 2-4% in ADHD cohorts (amphetamine vs methylphenidate OR 1.57). This is offset by a trial meta-analysis (YMRS SMD -0.17) and a within-person national cohort showing no rise in mania-related hospitalization and fewer psychiatric and substance-related admissions when stimulants were added to mood stabilizers. Cannabis is associated with psychosis risk, symptom exacerbation, more manic episodes and rapid cycling, and with attentional deficits that partially remit with abstinence; the one direct test of an ADHD-specific cannabis-psychosis interaction was null. Medication-induced activation is poorly delimited from mood episodes, so medication remains a mandatory competing explanation. Nicotine appeared only as a comorbid diagnosis, leaving H8.4 indeterminate. Trait-like attention deficits in nominally euthymic individuals were fully attenuated by current mania severity, so trait attribution needs repeated measurement with verified mood, sleep and substance status. No diagnosis or treatment guidance is offered.


### H8.1 — Mood episodes can produce episodic executive, speech, salience, and impulse-control changes.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** A meta-analysis of drug-free polysomnography (42512516) found objectively different sleep profiles by mood polarity: reduced total sleep time and efficiency in both mania and depression, reduced delta sleep only in depression, and reduced REM time with shortened REM latency and increased REM density in mania, indicating measurable state-dependent neurophysiology. A dimensional study in euthymic and at-risk individuals (42262032) found that attention/context-discrimination deficits were statistically accounted for by current mania severity, which is direct evidence that mood state contributes to measured cognitive performance. (PMID 42512516, PMID 42286971, PMID 41404486)
- **Strongest opposition:** No included record measured speech rate, salience attribution or impulse control within the same individuals across episodes and euthymia, so the specific claim about episodic speech and salience change rests on inference. All supporting designs are cross-sectional case-control or dimensional and cannot establish that an episode produced the change rather than that impaired sleep, medication or substance exposure produced both the episode and the deficit (42512516, 42262032). (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Mood state plausibly and measurably modifies sleep physiology and attentional performance, and substance exposure is associated with more manic episodes and rapid cycling (42286971); medication-induced activation can present with an overlapping symptom picture (41404486). The narrower proposition that episodes produce discrete executive, speech, salience and impulse-control changes is consistent with this evidence but not directly demonstrated by it. (PMID 42262032)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Sleep loss during an episode, rather than mood elevation itself, drives cognitive and behavioural change; Residual subsyndromal symptoms in nominally euthymic periods create apparent state contrasts; Concurrent cannabis, stimulant or nicotine exposure produces the activation attributed to mood; Medication initiation or change produces activation misread as an episode; Retrospective recall of episodes is reconstructed around a coherent narrative
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Cross-sectional case-control designs cannot establish temporal direction; Mood state assigned by clinician rating without blinding to cognitive results; No objective speech, salience or impulsivity measures in any included record; Numeric effect sizes for the polysomnography contrasts absent from the abstract; Medication status confounded with illness severity
- **Calibrated claim:** Mood state in bipolar-spectrum illness is associated with measurable differences in sleep architecture and in attentional/context-discrimination performance, and current manic symptom severity can statistically account for deficits that appear trait-like. Whether episodes specifically produce discrete changes in speech, salience and impulse control is plausible but not directly established by the abstract-level evidence retrieved here.
- **Falsifier:** Repeated within-person measurement showing that objective attention/inhibition performance, speech-rate measures and salience ratings do not differ between prospectively documented episodes and verified euthymia, once sleep duration, medication and substance exposure are held constant, would materially weaken the claim. Longitudinal data showing that apparent episodic change is fully explained by concurrent sleep loss would similarly weaken it.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Prospective daily mood charting with sleep duration and quality, energy, irritability, speech pressure, impulsivity and unusual certainty; clinician and collateral observations during at least one documented episode and one documented euthymic interval; and repeated brief objective attention/inhibition testing timed to those intervals, with a concurrent substance and medication timeline.


### H8.2 — Cannabis or THC can worsen cognition, anxiety, psychosis risk, or mood stability.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** A systematic review of bipolar disorder with specific substance use disorders (42286971) found cannabis use disorder frequently associated with increased manic episodes, rapid cycling, psychosis and earlier illness onset. A review of THC and schizophrenia (41127784) reported higher schizophrenia risk with high-THC cannabis, especially with familial vulnerability and regular adolescent use, and exacerbation of positive, negative and cognitive symptoms in established illness. A cessation review (41872072) found attention and executive deficits persisting beyond acute abstinence, with less complete recovery in adolescent-onset and heavy users. (PMID 41127784, PMID 42286971, PMID 41872072)
- **Strongest opposition:** A systematic review restricted to people with ADHD (41916924) found very little evidence that cannabis-associated psychosis risk is heightened in ADHD, with two of three studies suggesting no ADHD-specific effect and one finding comparable risk in ADHD and non-ADHD groups. The cessation review (41872072) also challenges assumptions of permanent cannabis-related brain damage, reporting CB1 receptor normalization within about 4 weeks and substantial cognitive recovery within the first year. (PMID 41916924)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Cannabis, particularly high-THC preparations with regular or adolescent-onset use, is associated with psychosis risk, symptom exacerbation and a more unstable bipolar course, and with attentional and executive deficits that are at least partly reversible with abstinence. No included record provided an anxiety outcome, and the associations are observational and vulnerable to reverse causation, so 'can worsen' is better read as a consistent association with dose and potency gradients than as an established individual-level causal effect. (PMID 41872072)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Reverse causation: prodromal psychosis, mood instability or attentional difficulty prompts cannabis use; Confounding by co-used substances (alcohol, nicotine, stimulants) and by poly-substance patterns; Premorbid cognitive differences that predate cannabis exposure; Shared genetic or familial liability to both cannabis use and psychosis; Withdrawal or acute residual intoxication mistaken for enduring cognitive impairment
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Self-reported exposure without biological verification of dose or potency; Narrative synthesis with no pooled effect estimates in the key reviews; Very small number of studies addressing the ADHD-specific interaction; Heterogeneous abstinence definitions across cessation studies; No anxiety or mood-stability outcome in the retrieved cannabis records
- **Calibrated claim:** Cannabis use, especially high-THC and regular or adolescent-onset use, is consistently associated with increased psychosis risk, symptom exacerbation, and a more unstable bipolar course, and with attention and executive deficits that partially remit over weeks to a year of abstinence. Evidence specific to people with ADHD is currently too sparse to show heightened cannabis-related psychosis risk in that group, and no retrieved evidence addressed anxiety outcomes.
- **Falsifier:** Prospective within-person data showing no deterioration in cognition, mood stability or unusual salience during documented cannabis exposure relative to verified abstinence, with biologically confirmed exposure status, would materially weaken the claim for this individual. At the population level, adequately powered longitudinal studies finding no association between potency-adjusted THC exposure and mood or psychotic outcomes after control for premorbid symptoms would weaken it.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** A dated cannabis timeline including product type, potency and route, ideally with biological confirmation; repeated cognitive testing and mood/salience ratings during a documented abstinence period of at least four to twelve weeks compared with periods of use; and documentation of whether unusual certainty or perceived guidance episodes were temporally associated with THC exposure and reduced sleep.


### H8.3 — Prescription stimulants can produce state-dependent activation or mood effects.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** Direct
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** High
- **Strongest support:** A case-control study of 1374 cases and 2748 controls aged 16-35 (39262211) found past-month prescription amphetamine use associated with incident psychosis or mania (adjusted OR 2.68, 95% CI 1.90-3.77), with a dose gradient reaching a 5.28-fold increase above 30 mg dextroamphetamine equivalents, while methylphenidate showed no association (OR 0.91, 0.54-1.55). A meta-analysis of 16 studies (N=391,043) (40900605) found pooled occurrences of 2.29% for psychotic disorder and 3.72% for bipolar disorder after stimulant exposure, with amphetamines exceeding methylphenidate (OR 1.57, 1.15-2.16) and dose-related increases. (PMID 39262211, PMID 40900605, PMID 39624511)
- **Strongest opposition:** A meta-analysis of randomized and prospective studies in bipolar disorder (38670627) found no increase in Young Mania Rating Scale scores with prescribed psychostimulants versus placebo (SMD -0.17, 95% CI -0.40 to 0.06). A Swedish within-individual cohort of 17,971 people with bipolar disorder (41856665) found add-on stimulants associated with lower psychiatric (aHR 0.89), substance-related (0.75) and all-cause (0.90) hospitalization, with no increase in mania-related hospitalization (0.93, 0.72-1.20). The ISBD review (38433530) likewise observed no increased (hypo)mania risk alongside mood stabilizers. (PMID 38670627, PMID 41856665)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Prescribed stimulants carry a low absolute occurrence of psychosis or mania that is agent-specific (amphetamines > methylphenidate) and dose-dependent, but when used at guideline doses with concurrent mood stabilization the aggregate evidence does not show mood destabilization and may show reduced hospitalization. The claim is therefore supported for high-dose amphetamine exposure and for misuse contexts, and not supported for guideline-dose, mood-stabilized treatment. (PMID 38433530)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Confounding by indication: emerging prodromal symptoms prompt dose escalation before the outcome; Reverse causation, with early mania or psychosis increasing stimulant-seeking; Concurrent cannabis, nicotine or non-prescribed stimulant exposure driving activation; Sleep loss produced by evening dosing rather than the drug's direct mood effect; Selection effects in hospitalized control groups inflating observed odds
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Meta-analytic proportions without unexposed comparators and heterogeneity above 95%; Hospitalized-control selection bias acknowledged by the case-control authors; Trials excluded unstable patients and used mania scales insensitive to rare switch events; Register outcomes capture hospitalization, not symptom-level activation; Within-person designs do not remove time-varying confounding such as adherence
- **Calibrated claim:** Prescribed stimulants are associated with a low absolute occurrence of psychosis or mania (roughly 0.6% to 3.7% across ADHD cohorts), with higher occurrence for amphetamines than methylphenidate and a dose gradient at higher amphetamine equivalents. Pooled trial data and a national within-person cohort do not show increased manic symptoms or mania-related hospitalization when stimulants are added to mood stabilizers or antipsychotics in bipolar disorder.
- **Falsifier:** A well-powered mirror-image or within-person study in mood-stabilized bipolar patients showing no dose-dependent increase in psychosis or mania after amphetamine initiation, or a randomized trial in which prospectively rated activation, speech pressure and salience did not differ from placebo across the therapeutic dose range, would materially weaken the claim. For this individual, prospectively documented stable mood across a supervised dose change would weaken it.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** A dated stimulant timeline with agent, formulation, dose in amphetamine or methylphenidate equivalents and timing of doses; prospective mood, sleep and activation ratings before and after each dose change, changing one variable at a time under psychiatric supervision; documentation of any prior treatment-emergent activation; and concurrent cannabis and nicotine exposure records.


### H8.4 — Nicotine can produce acute activation and dependence-related state changes.

- **Verdict:** Indeterminate
- **Certainty:** Very Low
- **Evidence directness:** No Eligible Direct Evidence
- **Directness to this case:** Low
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** No record in the selected set measured nicotine as an exposure with activation, cognitive or mood-state outcomes. The nearest relevant observations are indirect: nicotine use disorder co-occurred in 343 of 1551 dual-diagnosis individuals who misused methylphenidate (39624511), and tobacco use disorder in bipolar disorder was linked to increased suicidality, hospitalizations and medication nonadherence in a systematic review that described this literature as underexplored (42286971). (None)
- **Strongest opposition:** The same records provide no comparator-based estimate of nicotine's acute activating or dependence-related state effects, and 42286971 explicitly identifies non-alcohol, non-cannabis substance evidence in bipolar disorder as sparse and methodologically weak, so there is no basis to affirm or refute the claim from this evidence set. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Nicotine appears in this evidence set only as a co-occurring diagnosis correlated with poorer bipolar course and with stimulant misuse, not as a measured exposure producing activation. Any claim about acute nicotine activation or dependence-related state change in this case is currently untested rather than supported or refuted, and the observed correlations are equally consistent with nicotine use being a marker of illness severity or of poly-substance exposure. (PMID 39624511, PMID 42286971)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Nicotine use is a marker of overall illness severity rather than a state modifier; Self-medication of attentional or affective symptoms drives nicotine use (reverse causation); Co-occurring cannabis, alcohol or stimulant use accounts for the associated outcomes; Withdrawal-related irritability and inattention, not nicotine's direct activation, produce state change; Sleep disruption from evening nicotine use mediates any observed effect
- **Methodological weaknesses:** No comparator-based nicotine exposure study in the eligible set; Raw counts without denominators or effect estimates in the dual-diagnosis review; Nicotine treated as a comorbid diagnosis rather than a dose-timed exposure; Cross-sectional and narrative designs vulnerable to reverse causation; Screening prioritised mood and cannabis or stimulant records, likely underrepresenting nicotine pharmacology
- **Calibrated claim:** This evidence set does not permit a conclusion about whether nicotine produces acute activation or dependence-related state changes. Nicotine or tobacco use disorder co-occurs with stimulant misuse in dual-diagnosis samples and with worse bipolar course indicators, but no eligible record measured nicotine exposure against activation, cognitive or mood outcomes.
- **Falsifier:** Within-person or randomized nicotine-challenge and abstinence studies in adults with ADHD or bipolar-spectrum illness showing no change in activation, irritability, attention or sleep relative to placebo or verified abstinence would refute the claim; comparable studies showing dose- and timing-dependent activation would support it. Either result would resolve the current indeterminacy.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** A dated nicotine timeline including product, dose, daily pattern and time of last use; systematic ratings of irritability, activation, attention and sleep during periods of typical use, reduced use and abstinence, changed one variable at a time under clinical supervision; and documentation of whether nicotine use escalates before or during periods of reduced sleep or mood elevation.


### H8.5 — Medication effects can mimic or amplify baseline neurodevelopmental traits.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** A systematic review of SSRI-induced activation versus manic switch in youth (41404486) found substantial symptom overlap and inadequate definitions, instruments and reliability, demonstrating that medication-induced states are difficult to distinguish from mood episodes in practice. Stimulant-associated psychosis and mania (39262211, 40900605) show that a prescribed medication can generate symptoms otherwise attributed to underlying illness, and drug-treated bipolar patients showed a different polysomnographic profile from drug-free patients (42512516), indicating that medication itself alters state-level measures. (PMID 41404486, PMID 39262211, PMID 40900605, PMID 42512516)
- **Strongest opposition:** A national within-individual cohort (41856665) found add-on stimulants associated with fewer psychiatric, substance-related and all-cause hospitalizations and no increase in somatic or mania-related admissions, and the ISBD review (38433530) found no increased (hypo)mania risk with ADHD medications alongside mood stabilizers, with methylphenidate and amphetamine salts reducing ADHD symptoms. These findings argue against the general expectation that medications will amplify baseline symptomatology when appropriately used. (PMID 41856665, PMID 38433530)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Medication effects can produce activation, psychosis or mania that phenomenologically overlaps with primary illness and can alter objective state measures, so medication is a genuine competing explanation that must be dated and excluded. However, the retrieved evidence does not show that medications amplify neurodevelopmental traits specifically; no included record measured medication effects on trait-level markers such as motor coordination, time processing or social communication in adults. (PMID 38433530)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** The medication is a marker of illness severity that would have worsened regardless (confounding by indication); Untreated underlying illness, not the drug, produces the observed activation; Concurrent sleep loss or substance exposure coincides with the medication change; Nocebo or expectancy effects after starting or changing medication; Withdrawal or discontinuation effects misattributed to the newly started agent
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Pediatric SSRI evidence extrapolated to an adult with different exposures; No included record measured medication effects on neurodevelopmental trait markers; Medication status observational and confounded with severity in the polysomnography synthesis; Register outcomes too coarse to detect symptom-level amplification; Absence of validated instruments and reliability data for activation constructs
- **Calibrated claim:** Medication-induced activation, psychosis or mania can closely resemble primary mood episodes and is poorly delimited by current definitions and instruments, and medication also alters objective state measures such as sleep architecture; medication is therefore a necessary competing explanation to date and exclude. Evidence that medications specifically amplify baseline neurodevelopmental traits was not identified, and stimulants under mood stabilization were associated with fewer hospitalizations rather than worsening.
- **Falsifier:** Prospective single-case data showing that symptom severity, activation and objective performance are unchanged across supervised medication starts, dose changes and washouts, with sleep and substance exposure held constant, would materially weaken the claim for this individual. At the population level, studies with validated activation instruments showing that medication-emergent states are reliably distinguishable from and non-overlapping with primary episodes would qualify it.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** A complete dated medication timeline (stimulants, antidepressants, mood stabilizers, antipsychotics) with doses, start and stop dates and any washouts; repeated standardized symptom, activation and side-effect ratings anchored to those dates; and single-variable changes reviewed with the treating psychiatrist, with collateral observation to offset self-report and no unprescribed changes.


### H8.6 — Sleep, mood, cannabis, stimulants, and nicotine can interact.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** Polysomnographic findings differed both by mood polarity and by medication status in the same synthesis (42512516), showing that sleep, mood and medication co-vary. In dual-diagnosis samples, methylphenidate misuse clustered with concurrent alcohol, cannabis and nicotine use disorders and with psychiatric symptom exacerbation (39624511), and substance-specific reviews found cannabis use disorder associated with more manic episodes and rapid cycling while other substances tracked hospitalization and nonadherence (42286971). Add-on stimulants were also associated with reduced substance-related hospitalization (41856665), indicating substance-medication interplay. (PMID 42512516, PMID 42286971, PMID 39624511, PMID 41856665)
- **Strongest opposition:** The one review that directly tested an interaction found very little evidence that cannabis-related psychosis risk is heightened in people with ADHD (41916924), with two of three studies suggesting no ADHD-specific effect. No included record formally tested multiplicative interaction among sleep, mood state and multiple substances, so co-occurrence and correlated risk are being read as interaction. (PMID 41916924)
- **Qualified interpretation:** The retrieved evidence shows co-occurrence and correlated risk across sleep, mood state, medication and substance exposure, and one direct test of an ADHD-by-cannabis interaction was null. The claim is therefore supported at the level of clinically important co-variation and confounding, but not at the level of a demonstrated statistical or mechanistic interaction effect. (None)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Additive rather than interactive effects of independent risk factors; A common third factor (illness severity, impulsivity) drives sleep loss, substance use and episodes; Reverse causation, with emerging episodes driving both sleep loss and substance escalation; Shared measurement source inflating apparent co-variation (self-reported exposure and outcome); Selection into clinical samples where multiple exposures co-occur by referral pathway
- **Methodological weaknesses:** No formal interaction or moderation testing in any included record; Sleep, substance and medication exposures rarely measured simultaneously; Observational designs with substantial residual confounding; Raw counts without denominators in the dual-diagnosis synthesis; Underpowered evidence base for the ADHD-by-cannabis interaction
- **Calibrated claim:** Sleep, mood state, medication and substance exposure co-vary and are jointly associated with clinical outcomes, so each must be measured together and treated as a potential confounder of the others. Formal evidence that these factors interact multiplicatively is largely absent, and the one direct test of an ADHD-by-cannabis interaction on psychosis risk was null.
- **Falsifier:** Prospective within-person data in which the effect of sleep loss on attention, irritability and salience is the same regardless of concurrent cannabis, nicotine or stimulant exposure, and vice versa, would weaken the interaction claim. At the population level, adequately powered studies testing and rejecting interaction terms among sleep, mood state and substance exposure would materially weaken it.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Concurrent daily records of sleep duration and timing, mood and activation ratings, and all substance and medication exposures, kept long enough to permit within-person analysis of joint and separate effects. Any test of one variable should change only that variable, must exclude intentional sleep deprivation or substance exposure, and should be planned with the treating psychiatrist.


### H8.7 — Stable persistence during euthymia, abstinence, and adequate sleep supports a trait-level component.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** A preregistered review of neuroimaging in adults aged 35 and older with clinical, symptomatic or genetic ADHD risk (42472451), with 11 of 13 studies at low risk of bias, found persisting fronto-striatal, fronto-parietal and limbic alterations with a possible compensatory frontal-parietal pattern, consistent with a persisting neurobiological substrate. A cannabis cessation review (41872072) found attention and executive deficits persisting beyond acute cessation, and less complete recovery in adolescent-onset and heavy users, indicating that some deficits outlast abstinence. (PMID 42472451, PMID 41872072)
- **Strongest opposition:** In nominally euthymic bipolar and at-risk individuals, dimensional associations between risk and context-discrimination performance disappeared after covarying current mania severity (42262032), showing that residual state variance can fully account for deficits that appear trait-like. The same cessation review (41872072) reported CB1 receptor normalization within about 4 weeks and substantial cognitive recovery within the first year, so persistence measured shortly after cessation may reflect incomplete washout rather than a trait. (PMID 42262032)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Demonstrating persistence during euthymia, abstinence and adequate sleep is a necessary but not sufficient basis for trait attribution: 'euthymia' defined only by clinical impression may retain enough residual mood elevation to account for the deficit, and abstinence periods shorter than several months may not exclude residual substance effects. Trait inference requires repeated measurement with concurrently documented mood, sleep and substance status rather than a single assessment during a nominally stable interval. (PMID 41872072, PMID 42262032)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Residual subsyndromal mood elevation or depression during nominal euthymia; Incomplete washout of cannabis or stimulant effects during short abstinence; Medication effects on cognition and motor performance mistaken for baseline traits; Chronic sleep restriction that is habitual and therefore invisible as a 'state'; Practice, expectancy or motivation differences across repeated testing sessions
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Cross-sectional designs cannot distinguish stable traits from repeated similar states; Euthymia and abstinence often defined without objective verification; Imaging group differences do not establish functional trait-level impairment; Small bipolar subgroup (n=27) and self-report lifetime risk measures in the key state-trait study; No included record controlled mood, sleep and substance status simultaneously
- **Calibrated claim:** Persistence of executive and attentional difficulty during objectively verified euthymia, abstinence and adequate sleep would strengthen a trait-level interpretation, and ADHD-associated brain alterations do appear to persist into mid-life. However, residual current mania severity can fully account for apparently trait-like attention deficits, and cognitive recovery continues for up to a year after cannabis cessation, so stability must be demonstrated across repeated occasions with concurrently documented mood, sleep and substance status rather than inferred from a single stable-appearing interval.
- **Falsifier:** If repeated objective testing during verified euthymia, biologically confirmed abstinence of several months and documented adequate sleep showed performance within normal limits, or if apparent persistence disappeared after adjusting for concurrent residual mood symptoms and sleep duration, the trait-level component would be materially weakened. Conversely, stable impairment across multiple such intervals would strengthen it.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Repeated standardized neuropsychological and impairment measures on at least three occasions during independently verified euthymia, documented abstinence and adequate sleep, with mood charting, sleep records and substance verification collected concurrently; plus childhood-onset documentation from school records, prior evaluations and collateral developmental history to establish a pre-substance, pre-mood-episode baseline.


## Module F: Daydreaming, rejection, anxiety, and emotional compensation

For H9, a meta-analysis of 40 studies (24,977 participants) shows maladaptive daydreaming co-occurs with loneliness, shame, dissociation, OCD features, ADHD, autistic traits, trauma and mood symptoms, but reports no effect sizes and rests on cross-sectional self-report. Boredom and an emotional-compensation function are supported only by analogy to compensatory technology-use models; no selected study tested whether fantasy regulates affect. Temporal direction is unestablished: the sole cross-lagged study found escape behaviour preceding absorption and bodily dissociation, bibliometric mapping shows near-absence of longitudinal work, and a seven-wave cohort found childhood imaginary friendship unpredictive. H12 evidence is stronger: longitudinal peer-victimisation reviews, adult ADHD stigma synthesis and school emotional-burden data converge on association with distress and self-rated functional burden. Anxiety mediation shows correct temporal ordering in two large cohorts but explains a modest share of effect; one exploratory analysis found many small mediators rather than one dominant pathway. Gene-environment correlation, where polygenic liability predicts reported bullying exposure, further limits directional inference. Nothing here is diagnostic or causal for any individual.


### H9.1 — Maladaptive or immersive daydreaming is associated with loneliness, rejection, shame, or boredom.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Direct
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** A meta-analysis of 40 studies and 24,977 individuals (PMID 39999955) reported positive associations between maladaptive daydreaming and loneliness, shame, negative affect, emotion-regulation difficulties and psychological distress, plus negative associations with self-esteem and self-efficacy. (PMID 39999955, PMID 40867127)
- **Strongest opposition:** No eligible record in this set reported a null association, but the supporting meta-analysis provides no effect sizes, and the only prospective record touching immersive fantasy (PMID 40932793) found no path from dissociative or fantasy constructs to later behaviour, indicating the association literature has not been stress-tested longitudinally. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Maladaptive daydreaming co-occurs with loneliness and shame at group level in cross-sectional self-report samples; boredom is supported only indirectly (PMID 40867127, where trait boredom drives compensatory technology use, not daydreaming), and rejection or bullying as specific antecedents was not tested in any selected record. (PMID 40932793)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Shared negative-affect variance inflating all self-report correlations; Common-method bias from single-informant questionnaires; Depression or dissociation as a third variable generating both fantasy and loneliness; Reverse direction: immersive fantasy withdrawal produces loneliness
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Constituent studies cross-sectional and self-report; No pooled effect sizes or heterogeneity reported in the abstract; Construct overlap between maladaptive daydreaming, dissociation and internalising symptoms; Boredom evidence relies on a different outcome construct
- **Calibrated claim:** Maladaptive daydreaming is consistently correlated with loneliness, shame and low self-esteem in cross-sectional samples; the magnitude is unquantified at abstract level, and links to rejection and boredom specifically remain untested.
- **Falsifier:** A large prospective study using a validated maladaptive daydreaming measure that finds no association with loneliness, shame or boredom after adjusting for depression, dissociation and trait negative affect would materially weaken the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Administer a validated maladaptive daydreaming measure with an immersion/impairment threshold, alongside concurrent daily ratings of loneliness, shame, boredom and mood, so that the association can be tested within-person rather than inferred from group-level literature.


### H9.2 — Daydreaming can function as emotional compensation or escape.

- **Verdict:** Indeterminate
- **Certainty:** Very Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** A systematic review of 28 studies (PMID 40867127) found trait boredom mediated and moderated the path from psychological vulnerabilities to problematic digital use, operating through fear of missing out, loneliness and low self-regulation, consistent with Compensatory Internet Use Theory; a three-wave study of 20,137 adolescents (PMID 41890162) showed distress mediating neurodevelopmental traits to an escape-type behaviour. (PMID 40867127)
- **Strongest opposition:** The only cross-lagged test involving immersive fantasy constructs (PMID 40932793) found the escape behaviour predicted later absorption and bodily dissociation rather than the reverse, which is incompatible with a simple distress-then-compensation model. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** No selected record measured whether daydreaming reduces distress or serves a regulatory function; the compensation account is theoretically framed and behaviourally analogous rather than empirically tested for fantasy itself. (PMID 39999955, PMID 40932793, PMID 41890162)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Daydreaming as a comorbid symptom of dissociation or depression rather than a coping act; Bidirectional escalation in which fantasy and distress mutually reinforce; Normative imagination misinterpreted retrospectively as coping; Behavioural addiction processes with reward, not compensation, as the driver
- **Methodological weaknesses:** No functional or momentary-assessment test of affect change before and after fantasy; Compensation inferred from theoretical model rather than measured; Escape-behaviour outcomes (gaming, social media) substituted for daydreaming; Self-report throughout
- **Calibrated claim:** An emotional-compensation function for daydreaming is theoretically plausible and consistent with compensatory-use models for related escape behaviours, but no eligible study in this set tested it directly; it should be treated as an untested hypothesis.
- **Falsifier:** Ecological momentary assessment showing that daydreaming episodes are not preceded by elevated negative affect and are not followed by affect improvement would falsify the compensation account.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Time-stamped momentary records of affect, loneliness and boredom immediately before, during and after daydreaming episodes, with parallel records for other activities, would show whether fantasy functions as regulation, escalation or neither.


### H9.3 — Temporal direction between adversity and daydreaming is established.

- **Verdict:** Unsupported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** High
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** High
- **Strongest support:** No selected record establishes an adversity-to-daydreaming temporal sequence; the closest prospective evidence (PMID 41890162) shows adversity-to-distress-to-escape-behaviour ordering, but for gaming rather than fantasy. (None)
- **Strongest opposition:** A bibliometric mapping of 141 maladaptive daydreaming publications (PMID 42044681) documents that longitudinal studies and randomised designs remain persistent gaps; the only cross-lagged study (PMID 40932793) found the opposite direction to that predicted; a seven-wave birth cohort (PMID 41428080) found childhood imaginary friendship unpredictive of later outcomes; and gene-environment correlation evidence (PMID 41933711) shows polygenic liability predicts reported bullying and abuse exposure (ORs 1.24-3.37), confounding any adversity-first sequence. (PMID 40932793, PMID 42044681, PMID 41428080, PMID 41933711)
- **Qualified interpretation:** The literature base is structurally incapable of supporting a temporal claim: it is dominated by cross-sectional designs, and where prospective data exist they either point the other way or address adjacent constructs. (None)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Genetic liability driving both adversity exposure and fantasy proneness; Reverse causation: immersive withdrawal generating later social adversity; Third-variable trait absorption present before any adversity; Retrospective narrative reconstruction creating an apparent sequence
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Near-absence of longitudinal daydreaming studies; Single available cross-lagged study is small and young-adult only; Retrospective adversity reporting subject to recall and mood-congruent bias; No study measured adversity and daydreaming at multiple matched timepoints
- **Calibrated claim:** Temporal direction between adversity and maladaptive daydreaming has not been established; the available prospective evidence is sparse, addresses adjacent constructs, and is partly confounded by gene-environment correlation.
- **Falsifier:** A multi-wave cohort demonstrating that increases in loneliness, rejection or shame reliably precede increases in validated maladaptive daydreaming, after adjustment for prior fantasy levels, mood and genetic liability, would establish the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Dated developmental records, school reports and collateral family history establishing when immersive fantasy first appeared relative to documented peer difficulty, combined with prospective concurrent logging of adversity and fantasy going forward.


### H9.4 — Normative fantasy, dissociation, compulsivity, trauma, or mood can better explain daydreaming.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Direct
- **Directness to this case:** High
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** The maladaptive daydreaming meta-analysis (PMID 39999955) found positive associations with dissociation, obsessive-compulsive disorder, ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, psychotic symptoms, traumatic experiences, depression and anxiety, showing that competing constructs are pervasively entangled with the fantasy construct; CDS meta-analytic data (PMID 42341946) show a strong depression association (r=0.56) for an inattention-adjacent construct. (PMID 39999955, PMID 42341946, PMID 41428080)
- **Strongest opposition:** The same meta-analysis argues maladaptive daydreaming behaves as a distinct condition not fully explained by existing psychopathology, and CDS associations survived control for ADHD inattention, indicating that the alternatives do not wholly subsume the phenomenon. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Alternatives are strong competitors rather than proven substitutes: the evidence establishes extensive comorbidity and construct overlap without discriminant-validity testing that would show which construct is primary in any given person. (PMID 40932793, PMID 42044681)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Maladaptive daydreaming as a genuinely separable condition with high comorbidity; Shared transdiagnostic dissociative or absorption trait underlying all measures; Depression driving both inner-focused cognition and impairment ratings; Measurement artefact from overlapping item content across scales
- **Methodological weaknesses:** No analytic control for symptom overlap in the CDS synthesis despite acknowledged phenomenological similarity; Cross-sectional self-report base; Same-informant inflation of effect sizes; Imaginary-friend cohort evidence uses a construct distinct from immersive daydreaming
- **Calibrated claim:** Dissociation, compulsivity, trauma exposure, ADHD/autistic traits and mood symptoms all correlate substantially with maladaptive daydreaming and remain viable competing explanations; no eligible study demonstrates that any one of them fully accounts for it, and normative imagination has not been shown to predict later pathology.
- **Falsifier:** Discriminant-validity work showing that maladaptive daydreaming retains independent associations with impairment after full statistical control for dissociation, OCD, depression and trauma would weaken the counterclaim; conversely, complete attenuation would strengthen it.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Structured assessment of dissociative experiences, obsessive-compulsive phenomena, trauma history and current mood state administered during a documented euthymic and abstinent period, so that fantasy can be evaluated separately from state effects.


### H12.1 — Anxiety, shame, rejection, or bullying are associated with functional burden.

- **Verdict:** Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** Direct
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** High
- **Strongest support:** A systematic review of 24 longitudinal studies (PMID 41464078) found robust prospective associations between peer victimisation and later depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress symptoms, low self-esteem and loneliness, amplified by persistent exposure; a systematic review of adults with ADHD (PMID 42137527) linked internalized stigma to functional impairment and poorer quality of life; and a 735-student study (PMID 40707015) showed elevated emotional burden in ADHD and/or autistic adolescents predicting depression and anxiety. (PMID 41464078, PMID 42137527, PMID 40707015, PMID 41890162)
- **Strongest opposition:** Effect sizes across the longitudinal victimisation literature vary widely and self-harm/suicidality findings were inconsistent; the ADHD stigma review found perceived stigma had non-significant associations with quality of life, and structural stigma was never directly tested against functional outcomes. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Association with distress and self-rated functional burden is well established; association with objectively measured occupational or academic performance is much less well evidenced, and most estimates come from adolescent rather than adult working populations. (None)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Shared genetic liability increasing both adversity exposure and symptom burden; Depression inflating both recalled adversity and rated impairment; Neurodevelopmental symptom severity driving both victimisation risk and functional difficulty; Reporting bias in single-informant designs
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Wide heterogeneity in victimisation operationalisation and follow-up length; Predominantly self-report exposure and outcome; Few adult-workplace outcome studies; No quantitative pooling in the reviews cited
- **Calibrated claim:** Bullying, rejection, shame-related stigma and anxiety are consistently associated with elevated psychological distress and self-reported functional burden across longitudinal and cross-sectional evidence, with heterogeneous magnitude and limited adult occupational data.
- **Falsifier:** Large adult cohorts showing that historical bullying, current stigma and anxiety carry no association with objectively measured occupational functioning once symptom severity and mood state are controlled would materially weaken the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** A dated adversity timeline with collateral corroboration, standardised anxiety and shame/rejection-sensitivity measures, and objectively documented work-performance indicators to distinguish distress burden from performance burden.


### H12.2 — Anxiety or rejection-related distress mediates avoidance or social dysfunction.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** In a three-wave study of 20,137 adolescents (PMID 41890162), peer bullying victimisation and depressive symptoms serially mediated approximately one-third of the ADHD-to-escape-behaviour effect; in ALSPAC (PMID 40977579), mid-childhood anxiety significantly mediated childhood autistic traits to adolescent emotional/restrained eating (indirect b=0.08, 95% CI 0.02-0.13). (PMID 41890162, PMID 40977579, PMID 40707015, PMID 42137527)
- **Strongest opposition:** In 72 adults with structured-interview ADHD (PMID 42147013), executive function dominated symptom prediction (beta 0.64 and 0.47) while childhood trauma, resilience and parenting contributed minimal additional variance; and an exploratory 14-mediator analysis (PMID 41789525) found only two significant mediators, concluding that many mediators of small effect are involved rather than a dominant anxiety pathway. (PMID 42147013)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Anxiety- and rejection-related distress mediates part of the pathway from neurodevelopmental traits to some outcomes, with modest proportion mediated; the mediated outcomes studied are eating behaviour, gaming and internalising symptoms, not the avoidance, defensiveness and social withdrawal specified in this formulation. (PMID 41789525)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Executive dysfunction directly producing both distress and avoidance; Depression rather than anxiety carrying the mediating effect; Confounding by shared genetic liability to both mediator and outcome; Statistical mediation without causal mediation in observational data
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Mediation estimated from observational data without experimental manipulation of the mediator; Outcome constructs substituted for the target constructs; Exploratory multi-mediator models without confirmatory replication; Self-report mediators and outcomes
- **Calibrated claim:** Anxiety and rejection-related distress account for a modest, statistically detectable portion of the link between neurodevelopmental traits and later symptom or behavioural outcomes, but no eligible study demonstrated mediation of avoidance, defensiveness or reassurance seeking specifically, and at least one adult study found environmental and distress variables largely non-contributory.
- **Falsifier:** Experimental or randomised reduction of anxiety and rejection sensitivity that produces no corresponding change in avoidance or social functioning would falsify the mediation claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Repeated within-person measurement of anxiety and rejection sensitivity alongside behaviourally defined avoidance events, with a single-variable intervention (for example targeted anxiety treatment) and pre-post functional tracking, holding other supports constant.


### H12.3 — Prospective evidence establishes the direction of this mediation.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** Moderate
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** High
- **Strongest support:** ALSPAC (PMID 40977579) separated exposure at age 7, mediator at age 10 and outcome at age 13, giving correct temporal ordering for anxiety mediation (indirect b=0.08, 95% CI 0.02-0.13); the 20,137-participant three-wave study (PMID 41890162) reported temporally specific serial mediation with sensitivity analyses; and 24 longitudinal victimisation studies (PMID 41464078) establish prospective exposure-to-outcome ordering with identified mediators. (PMID 40977579, PMID 41890162, PMID 41464078)
- **Strongest opposition:** Gene-environment correlation evidence across two cohorts (PMID 41933711) shows psychiatric polygenic scores predict reported exposure to bullying and abuse (ORs 1.24-3.37, replicated), meaning prospectively ordered adversity-to-outcome paths may be partly non-causal; the exploratory Millennium Cohort analysis (PMID 41789525) found only two of 14 candidate mediators significant. (PMID 41933711)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Temporal ordering has been demonstrated for some mediation pathways, which is necessary but not sufficient for causal direction; residual confounding by genetic liability and unmeasured family factors remains substantially unaddressed. (PMID 41789525)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Genetic liability generating both adversity exposure and later symptoms; Pre-existing subclinical symptoms preceding measured exposure; Shared method and informant continuity across waves; Selective attrition in long cohorts
- **Methodological weaknesses:** No randomisation or instrumental-variable identification of the mediator; Long inter-wave intervals may miss the true causal lag; Mediators measured by self- or parent-report; Genetically informed confounding rarely modelled in the mediation studies
- **Calibrated claim:** Prospective studies establish that adversity and anxiety precede later distress outcomes and support temporal ordering for specific mediation paths, but they do not establish causal direction, and genetically informed evidence indicates part of the adversity-outcome association reflects gene-environment correlation.
- **Falsifier:** Within-family or genetically informed mediation designs showing that the anxiety mediation path disappears once shared familial and genetic liability are controlled would overturn the directional claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Prospective dated logs of rejection-relevant events, anxiety ratings and functional outcomes at fine temporal resolution, plus family history and any available childhood records, so that within-person lead-lag relationships can be examined against the individual's own baseline.


### H12.4 — Functional difficulty can remain independently associated with neurodevelopmental traits.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Low
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** High
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** In adults with structured-interview ADHD (PMID 42147013), executive function remained the strongest predictor of symptom severity (beta 0.64 inattention; 0.47 hyperactivity-impulsivity) while environmental factors added minimal variance; emotional burden and emotion-regulation deficits each independently predicted depression and anxiety in 735 students (PMID 40707015); and CDS-internalizing associations persisted after controlling for ADHD inattention (PMID 42341946). (PMID 42147013, PMID 40707015, PMID 42341946)
- **Strongest opposition:** The ADHD stigma review (PMID 42137527) found symptom severity and internalized stigma positively correlated and jointly linked to impairment, so trait and distress contributions cannot be cleanly separated in cross-sectional data; the adult ADHD study is small (n=72) and measured symptom severity rather than real-world function. (None)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Neurodevelopmental trait measures retain independent statistical association with symptom severity and distress after accounting for some environmental and affective variables, but the outcomes tested are largely self-rated symptoms rather than objectively verified functional impairment. (PMID 42137527)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Shared self-report method inflating trait-outcome associations; Insufficient measurement of environmental variables producing residual confounding; Depression inflating both self-rated executive difficulty and impairment; Selection of clinic-referred samples with high trait severity
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Small adult sample with cross-sectional design; Self- or informant-rated executive function rather than performance-based testing; Symptom severity substituted for functional outcome; Retrospective measurement of childhood adversity and parenting
- **Calibrated claim:** Neurodevelopmental trait measures show statistically independent associations with symptom severity and distress after partial adjustment for adversity and affective variables, but the evidence is small-sample, cross-sectional and largely self-rated, so independence from environmental contribution is provisional.
- **Falsifier:** Adequately powered studies using performance-based executive measures and objectively documented functioning that show trait effects fully attenuate after adjustment for anxiety, stigma and adversity would contradict the qualification.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Performance-based neuropsychological testing plus collateral- and record-verified functional outcomes obtained during euthymia and abstinence, repeated across contrasting environments, to see whether difficulty persists when distress and environmental demand are low.


## Global model, null, and boundary claims


### G1 — Baseline traits and state or environmental modifiers interact.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** High
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** Several records show measured trait-level performance shifting with state: covarying current mania severity abolished dimensional associations between lifetime mania/psychosis risk and context-discrimination performance (42262032); polysomnographic profiles differed by mood polarity and again by medication status (42512516); within-person sleep variation tracked mood instability across six months in 370 bipolar patients (42320790); rest-activity instability preceded 6-month mania symptoms in mania-vulnerable youth (41646692). On the environmental side, accommodation and autonomy manipulation altered occupational and learning outcomes (41552173, 42411300) while baseline traits persisted across 18 years (42365682). (PMID 42262032, PMID 42512516, PMID 42320790, PMID 41646692, PMID 41870441, PMID 41552173, PMID 42411300, PMID 42293966, PMID 41397339, PMID 42365682, PMID 41856665)
- **Strongest opposition:** Formal interaction was rarely tested and, where tested, was null: a synthesis restricted to ADHD found essentially no evidence of heightened cannabis-associated psychosis risk specific to ADHD (41916924). Mood and subjective-state instability were unrelated to actigraphic circadian indices over 10 weeks (42464599). An exploratory 14-mediator analysis found many mediators of small effect rather than identifiable moderating pathways (41789525), and in 72 structured-interview adults executive function dominated symptom prediction (beta 0.64/0.47) while adversity, resilience and parenting added minimal variance (42147013). (PMID 41916924, PMID 42464599, PMID 41789525, PMID 42147013)
- **Qualified interpretation:** The evidence supports co-variation and demonstrable state-dependence of measured trait indices, not a tested multiplicative interaction. No eligible record modelled interaction terms among sleep, mood, substances and environment; several modules noted that severity, workload and burnout were never adjusted for. The defensible reading is that state and environmental factors substantially modify what is observed, so trait estimates obtained without documenting concurrent state are unreliable. (PMID 42297780, PMID 40801243, PMID 42132039)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Additive rather than interactive contributions of independent risk factors; A common third factor (illness severity, impulsivity) generating sleep loss, substance use and impairment; Reverse causation, with symptom severity driving sleep irregularity and environmental selection; Common-method bias where exposure and outcome share one informant; Selection into clinical or service-attending samples where exposures co-occur by referral pathway
- **Methodological weaknesses:** No formal interaction or moderation testing in any extracted record; Sleep, mood, medication and substance exposures rarely measured simultaneously; Cross-sectional designs predominate; temporal ordering usually unresolved; Persistence and outcome associations sometimes appeared only with one informant type; Effect sizes frequently unreported at abstract level
- **Calibrated claim:** Baseline neurodevelopmental traits and state or environmental factors demonstrably co-vary, and state factors such as current mood severity, sleep and medication measurably alter apparently trait-level performance; formal statistical interaction among these factors is largely untested in the eligible evidence.
- **Falsifier:** Adequately powered within-person data showing that the effect of sleep loss, mood state or environmental demand on attention, inhibition and real-world completion is invariant, and that trait estimates obtained during episodes match those obtained during verified euthymia, abstinence and adequate sleep, would weaken the claim. Rejection of prespecified interaction terms in well-powered cohorts would weaken it further.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Concurrent daily records of sleep duration and timing, mood and activation ratings, substance and medication exposure, and task-level performance (type, novelty, urgency, structure, completion, errors, effort), analysed within-person with lagged models; repeated objective attention and inhibition testing anchored to documented episode and euthymic intervals; collateral observation to offset self-report.


### G2 — Context-dependent compensation can alter observable impairment.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** High
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** Manipulating context changes observable impairment. In 5152 adults with ADHD, CBT targeting executive dysfunction improved global functioning (SMD 0.47 post-treatment, 0.67 at follow-up), strongest for occupational functioning, with symptom change not predicting functional gain (41936228). Organizational-skills training improved rated organizational skills (teacher g=0.54, 95% CI 0.17-0.91; parent g=0.83, 0.32-1.34) (28088557), and behavioral therapy reduced adult inattention while cognitive training and neurofeedback did not (32954909). Camouflaging in autistic females delayed identification despite present difficulty (42052784), and frontal hypoactivation with parietal hyperactivation was interpreted as compensatory recruitment (42472451). (PMID 41936228, PMID 28088557, PMID 32954909, PMID 41552173, PMID 42052784, PMID 42472451, PMID 42411300, PMID 40761448, PMID 41590022, PMID 28398986)
- **Strongest opposition:** The internal-compensation reading is weakly evidenced. In 115 overlap-controlled studies, inattention was associated with weaker rather than preserved measured intelligence, reasoning, inhibition and working memory (41989410), and higher childhood IQ predicted lower adult persistence (OR 0.99, 0.98-1.00) (40287009), both more consistent with genuinely lower burden than with concealed impairment. CBT added to medication lost its advantage by 6-9 months (38084075), an umbrella review of 134 reviews documented persistent cross-domain adverse outcomes including unemployment (42199907), and camouflaging-distress associations occurred equally in non-autistic samples (42311563). (PMID 41989410, PMID 40287009, PMID 38084075, PMID 42199907, PMID 42311563)
- **Qualified interpretation:** Externally provided context (therapy, accommodation, autonomy, structure) reliably alters observable functioning; that limb is supported. The narrower proposition that internal compensation preserves output at the price of measurable extra effort or instability was tested by no eligible record: effort, physiological load and output were never measured together. Achievement alone is therefore an ambiguous datum, compatible both with successful compensation and with genuinely preserved functioning. (PMID 41089022, PMID 42132039, PMID 41808914)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Nonspecific effects of therapeutic contact, monitoring and attention rather than compensation; Achievement reflecting genuinely efficient functioning or favourable environments; Selection: workers and patients who obtain support may already be less impaired; Expectancy and reporter bias in unblinded rating-based outcomes; Retrospective attribution of remembered effort to a compensation narrative
- **Methodological weaknesses:** No record measured effort or performance instability alongside preserved output; Compensation inferred from neural activation patterns without behavioural effort measures; Parent-rated effects consistently exceeded teacher-rated effects in scaffolding trials; Camouflaging assessed largely by self-report with no achievement-independent criterion; Adult occupational redesign and job crafting were never tested experimentally
- **Calibrated claim:** Context and external support demonstrably alter observable impairment in ADHD and autistic adults, with moderate effect sizes for structured psychological intervention; evidence that internal compensation preserves achievement at a measurable effort cost is absent, so achievement alone cannot establish or exclude masked impairment.
- **Falsifier:** Within-person studies measuring effort, physiological load and output showing that high-achieving adults with neurodevelopmental traits expend no more effort than matched controls for equivalent output, together with blinded-rater adult trials showing no advantage of executive-focused intervention over an equally structured active control, would weaken the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Prospective task-level logs pairing rated effort, time spent, errors and completion against matched output, compared across high- and low-support periods with one scaffold changed at a time; ability-referenced impairment assessment (performance relative to measured capacity) rather than population norms; documentation of hidden supports, extensions or informal help used to achieve past outcomes; collateral or supervisor ratings alongside self-report.


### G3 — One coherent adaptive-constraint model explains the observations better than alternatives.

- **Verdict:** Unsupported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** No Eligible Direct Evidence
- **Directness to this case:** High
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** High
- **Strongest support:** No eligible record performed the comparative model test this claim asserts. The nearest supportive signal is that the 18-year prospective follow-up found adults with childhood ADHD impaired across more than 60% of outcomes while unaffected siblings resembled controls (42365682), which is consistent with a specific developmental trajectory rather than nonspecific adversity, and that overlap-controlled analysis showed ADHD symptom dimensions retain partly dissociable cognitive profiles (41989410). Neither addresses whether one integrated model outperforms alternative formulations. (None)
- **Strongest opposition:** Non-specificity is pervasive. Oculomotor markers were shared across ADHD, autism, DCD, OCD, borderline personality disorder and psychosis (41121040); exploration-exploitation and reward disturbances occurred across anxiety, mood, OCD, schizophrenia and addiction as well as ADHD (40287643); autistic traits shared genetic liability with internalising symptoms (rA=0.42) (42508582); adult autism and borderline personality disorder overlap enough to compromise diagnostic accuracy (42319023); medication-emergent activation is not reliably separable from mood episodes (41404486); current mania severity abolished apparently trait-like attention deficits (42262032); and polygenic liability predicted reported adversity exposure (41933711). (PMID 41121040, PMID 42508582, PMID 40287643, PMID 42319023, PMID 41638598, PMID 41789525, PMID 42341946, PMID 41404486, PMID 42262032, PMID 41933711, PMID 41916924)
- **Qualified interpretation:** This verdict means the claim is not established as a superior explanation, not that its components are false. Group-level literature cannot in principle adjudicate comparative explanatory adequacy for one person's life course (see G6), and where component claims were tested the results favoured shared mechanisms, plural formulations and live alternatives. Advancing a single unifying model at this stage would exceed the evidence. (PMID 41989410, PMID 41388592, PMID 42365682)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Multiple co-occurring conditions each contributing independently; One primary condition with secondary features mimicking additional diagnoses; Shared transdiagnostic liability generating correlated but non-causal phenotypes; State-dependent mood, sleep, medication and substance effects superimposed on a narrower trait set; Narrative coherence bias making a compelling life story feel more causal than data justify
- **Methodological weaknesses:** No record compared competing formulations head to head; Instrument item overlap inflating apparent construct convergence; Very few studies of co-occurring phenotypes (only two in one relevant review); Expert-opinion sources used where empirical comparison was unavailable; Component claims assessed with heterogeneous outcomes that cannot be pooled
- **Calibrated claim:** There is no eligible evidence that a single coherent adaptive-constraint model explains the observations better than plural or alternative formulations; several individual components are provisionally supported, but the unifying comparative claim should not be advanced.
- **Falsifier:** Studies that sequentially assess ADHD, autism, DCD and bipolar-spectrum illness in the same adults and show that one integrated constraint-plus-mismatch formulation predicts functional outcomes better than a plural model, or that apparently comorbid features remit when the single formulation is treated, would support the claim.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Sequential differential assessment for ADHD, autism, DCD and bipolar-spectrum illness with structured developmental interview and family collateral; school records and prior evaluations establishing childhood onset before any mood episode, substance exposure or occupational stressor; prospective observation during documented euthymia and abstinence; and explicit clinician-led comparison of a single-model against a plural formulation.


### G4 — Retrospective reconstruction and confirmation bias can create apparent coherence.

- **Verdict:** Partially Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** High
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** Moderate
- **Strongest support:** Retrospective reports are demonstrably shaped by current status. Across two cohorts, psychiatric polygenic scores predicted reported exposure to bullying and abuse (ORs 1.24-3.37, replicated), so recalled adversity partly indexes liability rather than environment (41933711). Persistence associations appeared only with parent-reported data and were eliminated by adjustment (40801243), and same-informant designs inflated effect sizes in meta-regression (42341946). A seven-wave birth cohort found childhood imaginary friendship unpredictive of later outcomes, a developmental null against a retrospectively appealing link (41428080). Claims about neurodivergent strengths in workplaces rested on anecdote, professional opinion and lived experience rather than empirical data (42132039, 41548993). (PMID 41933711, PMID 40801243, PMID 42341946, PMID 42147013, PMID 41428080, PMID 42132039, PMID 41548993, PMID 42311563, PMID 41755570, PMID 41680598)
- **Strongest opposition:** Prospectively ascertained data show the continuity is not purely reconstructed: the 18-year cohort recruited on childhood ADHD before any adult outcome was known and found broad impairment with unaffected siblings resembling controls (42365682); a national cohort used register and standardised instrument ascertainment at age 12 (41638598); and objective actigraphy predicted symptoms one year later without reliance on recall (41870441). (PMID 42365682, PMID 41638598, PMID 41870441)
- **Qualified interpretation:** The claim is a methodological caution rather than a tested empirical finding: no eligible record experimentally manipulated retrospective framing. What the evidence does establish is that informant, method and genetic liability systematically shape retrospective reports, and that self-reported strengths and masking narratives in this literature are largely testimonial. Reconstruction is therefore a live partial explanation, not a demonstrated primary one. (PMID 40972904, PMID 41089022)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Genuine developmental continuity accurately recalled; Mood-congruent recall shifting with current state rather than a stable identity narrative; Selective attention to confirming memories without wholesale reconstruction; Delayed recognition through service access and historical diagnostic practice rather than reinterpretation; Identity-affirming self-report inflating strengths while impairment recall remains accurate
- **Methodological weaknesses:** No experimental or blinded test of retrospective bias in the eligible set; Retrospective childhood trauma, parenting and symptom recall throughout the observational literature; Testimonial and case-based sources dominate the strengths evidence; Diagnostic labels themselves unstable over 24 months in one cohort; Absence of collateral or record-based corroboration in most included studies
- **Calibrated claim:** Retrospective reports in this literature are systematically shaped by informant, measurement method and genetic liability, and self-reported masking and strengths accounts are largely testimonial, so retrospective reconstruction and confirmation bias remain plausible partial contributors to apparent coherence; no eligible record tested them directly.
- **Falsifier:** Contemporaneous documentation (school records, teacher reports, prior evaluations, dated clinical notes) corroborating childhood-onset impairment independently of current self-report, together with collateral informants blind to the current formulation, would substantially reduce the weight of this counterclaim for a given case.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Structured developmental interview with family collateral obtained where safe; school records, report cards and any prior psychoeducational or clinical evaluations; a dated timeline of mood episodes, substances, medications and occupational stressors compared against documented symptom onset; and prospective rather than recalled logging going forward, with clinician and collateral observations balancing self-report.


### G5 — Multiple independent or interacting causes can outperform one unifying model.

- **Verdict:** Supported
- **Certainty:** Moderate
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** High
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** High
- **Strongest support:** Multiplicity is the norm rather than the exception. In a near-complete national cohort at age 12, 59% of those with moderate/severe neurodevelopmental disability had at least two co-occurring conditions, with ASD 14.9%, ADHD 21.2% and DCD 29.4% versus 2.4%, 8.9% and 5.7% in term controls (41638598). Candidate markers were shared across six diagnostic groups (41121040); motivational disturbances spanned anxiety, mood, OCD, schizophrenia and addiction (40287643); autistic traits and internalising symptoms shared genetic liability (rA=0.42) (42508582); cognitive disengagement tracked depression at r=0.56 independently of ADHD inattention (42341946); and a 14-mediator analysis concluded many mediators of small effect operate rather than one dominant pathway (41789525). (PMID 41638598, PMID 41121040, PMID 42508582, PMID 42319023, PMID 40287643, PMID 42341946, PMID 41789525, PMID 40801243, PMID 42286971, PMID 41404486, PMID 41579913, PMID 42147013)
- **Strongest opposition:** Overlap is not indistinguishability. With symptom overlap statistically controlled, ADHD-inattention, hyperactivity-impulsivity and cognitive disengagement showed partly dissociable cognitive profiles, including opposite directions of association with motor coordination (41989410). The 18-year cohort's sibling comparison group functioned like controls, indicating some specificity to childhood ADHD rather than diffuse multi-causality (42365682), and expert review advises confirming that one condition does not better explain the presentation before adding diagnoses (41388592). (PMID 41989410, PMID 42365682)
- **Qualified interpretation:** The claim is modal ('can outperform') and is well supported at group level by co-occurrence rates, shared markers and shared aetiology. It does not establish which combination applies to any individual, and it does not license abandoning parsimony: constructs remain separable when overlap is controlled, and cohort multimorbidity rates come partly from high-risk perinatal populations. (PMID 41388592, PMID 41916924, PMID 42120904)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Instrument item overlap generating artefactual co-occurrence; Ascertainment and referral bias inflating comorbidity in clinical samples; One primary condition with secondary features resembling additional diagnoses; Shared perinatal or genetic liability producing correlated but non-causal phenotypes; Diagnostic label inertia and crisis-setting labelling in record-based cohorts
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Key prevalence data from an extremely preterm rather than general population; Trait-level and register-based diagnostic ascertainment in several records; Narrative reviews without pooling or matched diagnostic comparisons; Very sparse adult multimorbidity data across modules; Exploratory multi-mediator modelling without confirmatory replication
- **Calibrated claim:** Co-occurrence across neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions is common, candidate markers are largely non-specific, and shared liability is documented, so a plural formulation involving multiple independent or interacting causes can plausibly outperform a single unifying model; which combination applies in an individual case cannot be determined from this evidence.
- **Falsifier:** A careful sequential differential assessment in which one diagnosis parsimoniously accounts for the full lifetime symptom set, with apparently comorbid features remitting when that condition is treated, would weaken the plural account for that case; population-level demonstrations that apparent comorbidity is fully explained by instrument overlap would weaken it generally.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Sequential differential assessment for ADHD, autism, DCD and bipolar-spectrum illness plus structured screening for anxiety, trauma, personality and substance-related contributions; collateral developmental history and records establishing which difficulties predate which exposures; and repeated standardised measures across a documented euthymic, abstinent interval to see which features persist and which remit.


### G6 — Group-level literature cannot establish individual diagnosis or causation.

- **Verdict:** Supported
- **Certainty:** High
- **Evidence directness:** Indirect
- **Directness to this case:** High
- **Confidence that wording is appropriately calibrated:** High
- **Strongest support:** Primary authors repeatedly disclaim individual-level inference, and the data show why. A meta-analysis of 391,043 individuals reported stimulant-associated psychosis and bipolar occurrence with I-squared above 95% and no unexposed comparators, stating causality cannot be established (40900605). Adult functional outcomes after childhood ADHD were heterogeneous (41985649); psychiatric diagnostic labels shifted markedly over 24 months (41755570); polygenic scores explained roughly 0.3% of variance (42297780) and small variance for adversity exposure (41933711); executive-function cluster membership had only moderate stability with effects of r=0.06-0.11 (42336134); and covarying current mania severity abolished apparently trait-like deficits (42262032). (PMID 40900605, PMID 41755570, PMID 42262032, PMID 41933711, PMID 42336134, PMID 41916924, PMID 42199907, PMID 42286971, PMID 41989410, PMID 42297780)
- **Strongest opposition:** Group data are not uninformative about individuals: multimodal models predicted adult global functioning after childhood ADHD well above chance (AUC 0.816) (41985649), and base rates from population cohorts legitimately shift individual prior probabilities (41638598). The correct inference is that group-level evidence constrains individual probability without determining diagnosis or causation. (PMID 41985649)
- **Qualified interpretation:** This is an epistemic boundary rather than an empirical hypothesis, and it is affirmed both in principle and by the observed heterogeneity, small effect sizes, diagnostic instability and state-dependence in the eligible evidence. Group findings can generate and rank hypotheses and identify safety-relevant modifiers; they cannot substitute for structured assessment, collateral history, records and longitudinal observation. (PMID 41638598, PMID 42472451)
- **Alternative explanations (unverified hypotheses unless cited):** Well-validated individual prediction models could narrow, though not eliminate, the inferential gap; High-penetrance or near-deterministic associations would permit stronger individual inference in other domains; Ecological fallacy misread as total non-informativeness of group data; Base-rate reasoning mistaken for individual causal attribution
- **Methodological weaknesses:** Group-level designs throughout, with no single-case experimental or n-of-1 evidence in the eligible set; Small effect sizes and wide confidence intervals limiting individual discrimination; Referral, service-attending and high-risk sampling restricting generalisability; Laboratory task performance with limited ecological validity for real-world functioning; Outcome and exposure often measured by the same informant
- **Calibrated claim:** Group-level literature cannot establish individual diagnosis or causation; it can rank hypotheses, identify safety-relevant modifiers and inform prior probabilities, and the observed heterogeneity, small effects, diagnostic instability and state-dependence in this evidence base reinforce that limit.
- **Falsifier:** Externally validated individual-level prediction or biomarker evidence with high sensitivity and specificity, replicated in independent cohorts and demonstrated to identify causal mechanisms in single cases, would narrow this boundary; nothing in the eligible evidence approaches that standard.
- **Personal or clinical data needed:** Standardised adult ADHD assessment with cross-setting impairment ratings; autism- and DCD-focused assessment where indicated with performance-based motor testing; mood and sleep charting with a dated substance and medication timeline; repeated prospective task-level and neuropsychological measures during verified euthymia and abstinence; clinician and collateral observations; and integration by the treating psychiatrist rather than by literature alone.


## Safety-relevant evidence-review considerations

- Prescription amphetamine exposure showed a dose-graded association with incident psychosis or mania (adjusted OR 2.68, 95% CI 1.90-3.77; 5.28-fold above 30 mg dextroamphetamine equivalents) while methylphenidate was null (OR 0.91, 0.54-1.55); this is agent- and dose-specific review evidence, not a treatment recommendation.
- Pooled stimulant-associated occurrence estimates (psychotic disorder 2.29%, bipolar disorder 3.72%) carry I-squared above 95% with no unexposed comparators, so they cannot be converted into individual risk figures; primary authors explicitly disclaim causality.
- Medication-emergent activation is poorly delimited from mood episodes: only 8 of 30 studies defined activation and no validated mania instrument existed. Unusual salience or certainty temporally associated with reduced sleep, mood elevation or substances warrants clinical evaluation, not self-review.
- High-THC cannabis was associated with psychosis risk and with more manic episodes and rapid cycling; the only direct test of an ADHD-specific cannabis-psychosis interaction rested on three studies and was underpowered, so absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
- The source protocol's prohibition on intentional sleep deprivation, substance exposure or unprescribed medication change is consistent with the evidence: rest-activity instability preceded 6-month mania symptoms in mania-vulnerable youth, and no eligible record establishes safe experimental sleep restriction in bipolar-spectrum illness.
- Cannabis-related cognitive recovery continues for weeks up to about a year after cessation, so short abstinence intervals do not license attributing residual executive difficulty to a stable trait.
- Trait attribution during nominal euthymia can be spurious: dimensional associations with attention performance disappeared after covarying current mania severity, indicating that residual subsyndromal elevation can masquerade as a trait.
- Digital-intervention reviews documented adverse effects including physical discomfort, emotional reactions and gaming addiction in 8 of 26 reviews, with harms inconsistently measured; any self-experiment with scaffolds should change one variable at a time and be reviewed with the treating psychiatrist.
- Longitudinal peer-victimisation reviews link adversity to post-traumatic symptoms, with less consistent self-harm and suicidality findings; a history of rejection and shame is a matter for clinical assessment rather than literature-based inference.
- Nothing in this review constitutes a diagnosis, an individual causal attribution, or a treatment directive; full-text screening, formal risk-of-bias assessment, human adjudication and clinical sign-off remain pending.

## Review limitations

- All screening, extraction and synthesis used inspected PubMed abstracts in the frozen packet; no full text was read, so effect estimates, adjustment sets, attrition and risk-of-bias detail are frequently recorded as NR and must not be treated as null.
- Rapid-review cap: a fixed ordered sample of 140 title/abstract records per module and 14 extractions per module, which displaced eligible records and left several claims (hyperfocus, interest-dependent attention, adult DCD persistence, nicotine as a dosed exposure) with no direct evidence.
- Databases specified in the protocol were unavailable to this reviewer: no Embase, PsycINFO, Web of Science, Scopus, Cochrane CENTRAL, ClinicalTrials.gov or WHO ICTRP searching, no citation chaining, and no grey-literature or guideline retrieval.
- No human Reviewer 2: this is an independent secondary AI reviewer's blinded pass with no dual human screening, no consensus adjudication and no third-reviewer arbitration.
- No clinical sign-off: the treating psychiatrist has not reviewed these judgements, and no individual diagnostic, causal or treatment inference is permitted.
- Design-appropriate risk-of-bias instruments (AMSTAR 2, RoB 2, ROBINS-I, QUIPS, QUADAS-2, Newcastle-Ottawa) and GRADE certainty grading were not applied; all quality labels are provisional abstract-level judgements with stated reasons.
- Retraction, expression-of-concern and major-correction status could not be verified without bibliographic tool access, and duplicate records were flagged but not formally deduplicated.
- The G1-G6 assessments were produced by re-synthesising the seven module outputs only; no dedicated global search was run, and no literature on comparative model fitting, formal interaction testing or single-case methodology was sought.
- Included evidence is heavily weighted toward paediatric, adolescent, autism-only or service-attending samples, limiting directness to an adult professional case with a reported bipolar history.
- No meta-analysis, heterogeneity quantification, publication-bias testing or sensitivity analysis was performed at the global level; synthesis is narrative and construct-specific.
- Embase, PsycINFO, Scopus, Web of Science, CENTRAL, and paid full texts were not independently searched by Claude.
- AI-AI agreement is not accuracy and cannot substitute for human adjudication.
